## CUAD: label count, how definitions are phrased, boundary rules, review process, difficulty by category

### Takeaway
CUAD has 41 label categories, each defined by a short name plus a one-or-two-sentence question (often with an explicit "This includes ..." scope sentence), backed by more than 100 pages of unpublished-in-the-paper annotation rules and a seven-step law-student-then-attorney review. Difficulty varies enormously by category: near 100% AUPR for some labels, about 20% for others.

### Cited Findings
- **[read]** 41 label categories, 510 contracts, 13,101 labeled clauses, 25 contract types, 9,283 pages. Labeled clauses make up about 10% of each contract on average; about 0.25% of each contract is highlighted per label. — [CUAD paper, arXiv 2103.06268](https://arxiv.org/abs/2103.06268)
- **[read]** Labels are grouped into three families: "General information" (party names, document names, dates, governing laws, license grants, renewal terms), "Restrictive covenants", and "Revenue risks". — [CUAD paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2103.06268)
- **[read]** Definition phrasing (Tables 4 and 5): each category is a name plus a description, written either as a noun phrase ("Document Name: The name of the contract"; "Parties: The two or more parties who signed the contract") or as a question ("Effective Date: On what date is the contract is effective?"; "Anti-Assignment: Is consent or notice required of a party if the contract is assigned to a third party?"). Several add an inclusion sentence: "Renewal Term: What is the renewal term after the initial term expires? This includes automatic extensions and unilateral extensions with prior notice."; "Cap on Liability: ... This includes time limitation for the counterparty to bring claims or maximum amount for recovery."; "Uncapped Liability: ... This also includes uncap liability for a particular type of breach such as IP infringement or breach of confidentiality obligation." — [CUAD paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2103.06268)
- **[read]** One label is defined purely by reference to other labels: "Competitive Restriction Exception: This category includes the exceptions or carveouts to Non-Compete, Exclusivity and No-Solicit of Customers above." — [CUAD paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2103.06268)
- **[read]** The definition text is reused verbatim as the model prompt: "Highlight the parts (if any) of this clause related to "<Label Category>". Details: <Label Category Description>". The answer is the span or the empty string. — [CUAD paper, Appendix A.3](https://arxiv.org/abs/2103.06268)
- **[read]** Task definition: "For each label category, we identify every clause in every contract that is most relevant to that label category." Models output start and end tokens. The format follows SQuAD 2.0 so that a question may have no answer. — [CUAD paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2103.06268)
- **[read]** Boundary-related statements the paper does make: "Because many legal clauses are very long and contain various sub-parts, sometimes only a sub-part of a sentence is responsive to a category." Released files may contain footers, page numbers and confidentiality legends; "Some sentences may not be relevant to the corresponding category. Some sentences may correspond to a different category." Redactions are kept and normalised in the answer column ("January __ 2020" becomes "1/[]/2020"). — [CUAD paper, Appendix A.2](https://arxiv.org/abs/2103.06268)
- **[read]** Span plus derived answer: for each label and contract CUAD also releases "supplementary annotations" determined from the extracted clauses, e.g. a yes/no answer for "Uncapped Liability". Volunteers added the Yes/No column by hand in the last step. — [CUAD paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2103.06268)
- **[read]** Training: law students had 70-100 hours of training (video instruction and live workshops with experienced lawyers, detailed instructions, quizzes, labeling sample contracts in eBrevia). Annotators "adhered to over 100 pages of rules and annotation standards". "Each annotation was verified by three additional annotators"; each page was reviewed at least 4 times at 5-10 minutes per page; estimated value over $2 million. — [CUAD paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2103.06268)
- **[read]** Seven-step process: (1) law student training; (2) law student label in eBrevia; (3) keyword search to capture categories missed in step 2; (4) category-by-category report review, where students export clauses and flag suspected mislabels; (5) attorney review of the report, with discussion to consensus; (6) "eBrevia Extras Review": a machine tool lists clauses it thinks are responsive but humans did not label, attorneys and students review all of them, repeated "until all or substantially all of the 'extras' are incorrect labels"; (7) final export. — [CUAD paper, Appendix A.2](https://arxiv.org/abs/2103.06268)
- **[read]** Best model (DeBERTa-xlarge): AUPR 47.8%, precision 44.0% at 80% recall, 17.8% at 90% recall. Performance "varies substantially by category, with some close to the ceiling of 100% AUPR and others much lower at only around 20% AUPR". The category chart orders labels from easiest to hardest roughly as: Governing Law, Document Name, Parties, Agreement Date, Expiration Date, Anti-Assignment ... down to Most Favored Nation, Rofr/Rofo/Rofn, IP Ownership Assignment, Covenant Not To Sue. — [CUAD paper, Figures 4 and 8](https://arxiv.org/abs/2103.06268)
- **[read]** Scoring allowance for short, repeated spans: the Parties label "often has several very small extracted segments of text in a given contract", so a prediction counts if the gold segment is a substring of it. A match otherwise uses Jaccard similarity between word sets. One label (Price Restrictions) had no test examples after the random split and was ignored. — [CUAD paper, Appendix A.1](https://arxiv.org/abs/2103.06268)
- **[read]** Training-set size mattered a great deal: the best and worst training-set sizes differ by 15.4 points of AUPR for RoBERTa-base. — [CUAD paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2103.06268)

### Inferences
- The easy CUAD labels are short, formulaic and occur once per document (law, name, dates); the hard ones are concept labels whose wording varies and whose extent is a matter of judgement. A tax-statute dictionary should expect the same split between "value" classes (rate, dollar amount, date) and "concept" classes (condition, exception).
- Writing each definition as a question plus an explicit "This includes ..." sentence serves both humans and models, because CUAD feeds the same text to both.
- CUAD's step 6 (machine-suggested extras reviewed by people until none are right) is a recall check that this project could copy cheaply: model-only spans go to an adjudication list.
- A class defined as "the exception to classes X, Y, Z" is precedent for an "exception" span class tied to a parent class by an attribute.

### Gaps
- The 100+ pages of CUAD annotation rules are not in the paper, and the repository README could not be fetched (the `main` and `master` raw paths returned a 14-byte error page). The exact span-boundary rules (whole sentence vs. sub-sentence, treatment of headings and cross-references) are therefore not documented here.
- The paper reports no inter-annotator agreement statistic (no kappa, no annotator F1).

## Other legal span datasets and their guidelines: MAUD, ContractNLI, LEDGAR/LexGLUE, LegalBench, SARA

### Takeaway
ContractNLI gives the clearest published span-boundary rule (fixed sentence or list-item units; evidence "as concise as possible" yet "self-contained"; non-contiguous allowed; all redundant evidence marked). SARA is the only tax-statute precedent: it treats each subsection as a predicate with slots, some of them filled implicitly or by earlier subsections. LegalBench has almost nothing on extracting values from statute text (3 of 162 tasks use statutory text).

### Cited Findings

**ContractNLI**
- **[read]** 607 annotated non-disclosure agreements, 17 fixed hypotheses, three document-level labels (Entailment, Contradiction, NotMentioned). Evidence is required only for Entailment or Contradiction. — [ContractNLI, arXiv 2110.01799](https://arxiv.org/abs/2110.01799)
- **[read]** Span unit is fixed in advance: "a span is a sentence or a list item within a sentence". Sentences are split with Stanza and then split again at inline list markers. A document averages 77.8 candidate spans and 2,254.0 tokens. — [ContractNLI](https://arxiv.org/abs/2110.01799)
- **[read]** The boundary rule, quoted: "Evidence spans therefore must be as concise as possible (need not be contiguous) while being self-contained, such that a reasonable user should be able to understand meaning just by reading the evidence spans". The worked example includes a lead-in paragraph "in order to clarify the clauses' subject". "We comprehensively identify evidence spans where they are redundant." — [ContractNLI](https://arxiv.org/abs/2110.01799)
- **[read]** Reason for whole units instead of token spans: "a lawyer can then check the evidence with comprehensible context around it". The authors also argue that predicting start and end tokens makes a model solve "span boundary detection and evidence identification concurrently, whereas the definition of spans is usually fixed for many applications"; their model does multi-label classification over the fixed spans instead. — [ContractNLI](https://arxiv.org/abs/2110.01799)
- **[read]** Hypotheses that only test the presence of a clause type were excluded. Because hypotheses are fixed, the authors "were able to utilize an example-oriented annotation guideline to improve annotation consistency". — [ContractNLI](https://arxiv.org/abs/2110.01799)
- **[read]** Process: a computational linguistics researcher (primary annotator) plus two Mechanical Turk workers chosen for consistent performance, who annotated redundantly "with a priority on coverage". Spans were merged, then the primary annotator reviewed them, judged labels and "consolidate[d] the span boundaries". Most of the development set and some of the test set were annotated by the primary annotator alone. Most entailed or contradicted hypotheses have one or two evidence spans; some have up to nine. — [ContractNLI, Appendix A.1](https://arxiv.org/abs/2110.01799)

**MAUD**
- **[read]** 152 merger agreements, 47,457 annotations: 8,226 extracted "deal point texts" and 39,231 question-answer pairs. 92 questions from the 2021 ABA Public Target Deal Points Study (which has 130), 22 deal point types, 7 categories. The deal point type decides which questions apply to a text. Multi-label questions were recast as binary, raising the effective question count from 92 to 144. — [MAUD, arXiv 2301.00876](https://arxiv.org/abs/2301.00876)
- **[read]** Two-step design: "First, lawyers extract key legal clauses from the contract (a span extraction task). Second, they interpret the meaning" by answering standardised multiple-choice questions. Some questions are conditional on the answer to another question. — [MAUD](https://arxiv.org/abs/2301.00876)
- **[read]** An "abridged" dataset (14,928 examples from 94 agreements) keeps only the text relevant to a question and marks skipped text with the delimiter `<omitted>`, i.e. a published convention for discontinuous spans. — [MAUD](https://arxiv.org/abs/2301.00876)
- **[read]** Process and agreement: over 10,000 hours; 70-100 hours of training per law student, quizzes and an instructions handbook; teams of three students annotate by consensus (text extraction and answers), escalate disagreements to an experienced M&A lawyer, and every annotation is then reviewed by a lawyer. "We unfortunately did not retain the records necessary for us to calculate inter-annotator agreement metrics. However, lawyers reviewing the student annotations report that they agreed about 80% of the time with student annotations." A post-hoc check by an outside attorney on 10 contracts disagreed with 3 of 440 gold labels, and on discussion accepted the gold label for all three. — [MAUD, Appendix A.12-A.13](https://arxiv.org/abs/2301.00876)

**LEDGAR and LexGLUE**
- **[read]** LEDGAR (Tuggener et al. 2020) is paragraph-level classification, not span extraction: about 850k contract provisions with 12.5k categories, where each label "represents the single main topic (theme)" of the provision. LexGLUE keeps 80k provisions and "only the 100 most frequent categories as a simplification", split by year (60k / 10k / 10k). — [LexGLUE, arXiv 2110.00976](https://arxiv.org/abs/2110.00976)

**LegalBench**
- **[read]** 162 tasks. By format: 112 binary classification, 35 multiple choice, 8 multi-class or multi-label, 7 open generation. By source text: contracts 55, merger agreements 34, plain English 32, legal opinions 11, "statutory text (3)". By reasoning type: interpretation 119, issue-spotting 16, rule-application 16, rule-conclusion 16, rhetorical understanding 10, rule-recall 5. — [LegalBench, arXiv 2308.11462](https://arxiv.org/abs/2308.11462)
- **[read]** Tasks on statute text: `sara_entailment` and `sara_numeric` (tax code), and `proa` (does a statutory clause contain a private right of action; 50 positive and 50 negative clauses written by hand "drawing inspiration from clauses found in different state codes"; negatives include clauses that empower a non-private party such as an attorney general). GPT-4 and GPT-3.5 both reach balanced accuracy of at least 90% on `proa`. — [LegalBench](https://arxiv.org/abs/2308.11462)
- **[read]** Tasks that extract a span or term: `definition_extraction` (extract the term being defined from a Supreme Court sentence; GPT-4 81.8%, Incite-Base 80.6%), the Securities Complaint Extraction tasks (party names; scored with F1 because several entities per sample), and `successor_liability`. None extracts a value from statute text. `rule_qa` asks for a rule and where it is codified, from memory, and was graded by hand. — [LegalBench](https://arxiv.org/abs/2308.11462)
- **[read]** Scoring practice for extraction: "we perform normalization on generated outputs to account for differences in tense/casing/punctuation". Classification output was cut at the first newline. — [LegalBench](https://arxiv.org/abs/2308.11462)
- **[read]** The paper notes that a cue that looks reliable for definitions is not: defined terms "often (but not always) appear in quotation marks or near a citation to a dictionary". — [LegalBench, Appendix F.8](https://arxiv.org/abs/2308.11462)
- **[read]** The CUAD-derived tasks in LegalBench are reframed from span extraction to clause-level yes/no classification, because CUAD "is intended for evaluating extraction with span-prediction models". — [LegalBench](https://arxiv.org/abs/2308.11462)

**SARA (US tax statutes)**
- **[read]** Statutes: nine Internal Revenue Code sections "well-supported by Treasury Regulations": §1 (tax on individuals), §2 and §7703 (marriage and other statuses), §152 (dependents), §63, §68, §151 (exemptions and deductions), §3301 and §3306 (employment). They were simplified to remove highly specific parts and so that sections refer only to sections in the subset; original numbering was kept with no renumbering ("there is a section 63(d) and a section 63(f), but no section 63(e)"). — [SARA, arXiv 2005.05257](https://arxiv.org/abs/2005.05257)
- **[read]** Structure: the statute tree has 132 leaves and 193 nodes; leaf depth min 1, max 6, average 3.6. Cross-references by subsection count: explicit within the section 30, explicit to another section 34, implicit within 25, implicit to another section 44. 62 sections refer to time; 50 state an exception or a substitution; 55 involve numerical reasoning. — [SARA](https://arxiv.org/abs/2005.05257)
- **[read]** The "slot" view of a provision: "Each section and its subsections may be cast as a predicate whose truth value can be checked against a state of the world." "Each subsection refers to a certain number of slots, which may be filled by existing entities". "Certain slots are implicitly filled: §7703(a)(1) and (b)(3) mention a 'spouse', which must exist since the 'individual' is married." Slots filled earlier in a section are reused later ("household" in §7703(b)(1), (b)(2), (b)(3)). "Sections are typically structured around a general rule, followed by a number of exceptions." — [SARA](https://arxiv.org/abs/2005.05257)
- **[read]** Cases: for each subsection, two hand-written cases, one where it applies and one where it does not, "vetted by a law professor for coherence and plausibility"; 176 train and 100 test entailment cases; a further 100 numerical cases (80 train, 20 test) asking how much tax is owed. — [SARA](https://arxiv.org/abs/2005.05257)
- **[read]** Formal side: each subsection is one Prolog rule; any term "appearing in a subsection and relevant across subsections is turned into an argument of the Prolog rule"; unfilled slots are unbound variables. Case facts use 61 predicates in neo-Davidsonian style (e.g. `marriage_(e)`, `agent_(e, alice)`, `start_(e, "1993-01-24")`). "The level of detail of these predicates is based on the granularity of the statutes themselves." The Prolog program scores 100% on the cases. — [SARA](https://arxiv.org/abs/2005.05257)
- **[read]** An example of a numeric rule that "needs to be parsed out of natural text": §1(c)(2), "$3,315, plus 28% of the excess over $22,100 if the taxable income is over $22,100 but not over $53,500". An example of an absolute time window: §63(c)(7), "In the case of a taxable year beginning after December 31, 2017, and before January 1, 2026". — [SARA](https://arxiv.org/abs/2005.05257)
- **[read]** The dataset's answers are not current law: "Because the statutes were simplified, the answers to the cases are not those that would be obtained with the current version of the IRC." — [SARA](https://arxiv.org/abs/2005.05257)

### Inferences
- ContractNLI's rule transfers directly to statutes: fix the candidate unit (the enumerated provision or list item), then let a span be the shortest text that still reads on its own, adding the lead-in when the subject would otherwise be missing.
- SARA's feature counts are a ready-made list of what a tax span dictionary must be able to mark: time references, exceptions and substitutions, numeric operations, explicit and implicit cross-references, and slots filled elsewhere. Implicit cross-references (44 to other sections) outnumber explicit ones (34), so a class for "term defined elsewhere" matters as much as one for citations.
- MAUD and CUAD both pair a verbatim span with a normalised answer drawn from a closed list. That is the "span plus attribute" pattern.
- No public benchmark tests extraction of rates, amounts or dates from statute text, so this project's own hand marks are the only reference.

### Gaps
- LEDGAR's own paper and guidelines were not read; the figures above are LexGLUE's description of it.
- SARA has no span annotation of the statute text itself; its structure is in Prolog, so it offers a slot model, not boundary rules.

## Biagioli/Francesconi provision model: provision types and their arguments

### Takeaway
The model's shape is confirmed (provision types, each with named arguments; two groups, "Rules" and "Rules on Rules"/amendments; two software modules, one to classify a paragraph by type and one to extract its arguments), but the full type-by-argument table could not be read in this session: every host carrying the papers refused the fetch. What follows is from search summaries and must be checked against the papers before it is relied on.

### Cited Findings
- **[search]** Biagioli, Francesconi, Passerini, Montemagni and Soria, "Automatic semantics extraction in law documents", ICAIL 2005, pages 133-140. The abstract describes a metadata scheme of "provision types and their arguments", with two modules: one assigns text fragments to provision types, one extracts their arguments. The stated aim is to support manual annotation of normative texts. — [Crossref record](https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1145%2F1165485.1165506); [IRIS UniTrento record](https://iris.unitn.it/handle/11572/21160)
- **[search]** Francesconi and Passerini, "Automatic Classification of Provisions in Legislative Texts", Artificial Intelligence and Law 15, pages 1-17 (2007): a bottom-up strategy that qualifies existing texts by provision type, classifying paragraphs with Naive Bayes and SVM; a secondary summary says SVM performed better and that the work used "the same eleven functional types". — [Springer record](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10506-007-9038-0); [PhilPapers record](https://philpapers.org/rec/FRAACO)
- **[search]** The model divides provision types into two groups: "Rules" (constitutive, e.g. Definition; regulative, e.g. Duty, Right, Power, Permission-style concepts, Procedure) and "Rules on Rules" (amendments, e.g. Insertion, Abrogation, Substitution). Amendments are described in one summary as Temporal, Extension or Content amendments. — [Francesconi, "An Approach to Legal Rules Modelling and Automatic Learning", JURIX 2009](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.5555/1671082.1671091); [Semantic Web Journal paper swj615](https://www.semantic-web-journal.net/system/files/swj615.pdf)
- **[search]** Arguments are type-specific properties, "such as the Bearer or the Counterpart of a Right". A different, later paper lists "Obligation: Addressee, Action, Third-party" and a prohibition with Action and Third-party; the search summary warns that this is not a direct statement of Biagioli's own table. — [Semantic Web Journal, "Semantic model for legal resources"](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.3233/SW-140150); [CLiC-it 2021 obligations extraction paper](https://aclanthology.org/2021.clicit-1.57.pdf)
- **[search]** Origin: Biagioli (ITTIG-CNR) proposed viewing a legislative text as a set of provisions expressed by speech acts; cited source is Biagioli 1997, "Towards a Legal Rules Functional Micro-ontology" (LEGONT '97). — [Jusletter IT article](https://author.weblaw.ch/magnoliaAuthor/jusletter-it/en/issues/2011/104/article_230.html)
- **[search]** A related Italian system (Soria, Bartolini, Lenci, Montemagni, Pirrelli) classifies law paragraphs by regulatory content and extracts text fragments for the semantic roles tied to each type. — [Semantic Scholar record](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/Automatic-Classification-and-Analysis-of-Provisions-Bartolini-Lenci/05fc0715bae645a0423d0d81f5682f29d42d2a8b)
- **[search]** Annotation schemes for amendments: Akoma Ntoso's `textualMod` element has a required `type` with the values repeal, substitution, insertion, replacement, renumbering, split, join. Its siblings under `activeModifications` are `meaningMod`, `scopeMod`, `forceMod`, `efficacyMod` and `legalSystemMod`. A modification is "active" in the amending document and "passive" in the amended one. — [OASIS Akoma Ntoso schema, TextualMods type](https://docs.oasis-open.org/legaldocml/akn-core/v1.0/os/part2-specs/os-part2-specs_xsd_Simple_Type_TextualMods.html); [OASIS activeModifications element](https://docs.oasis-open.org/legaldocml/akn-core/v1.0/csd01/part2-specs/material/AkomaNtoso30-csd13_xsd_Element_activeModifications.html)

### Inferences
- The model is exactly the "provision type with required and optional classes" design: classify the unit first, then the type fixes which argument spans to look for. MAUD does the same thing (deal point type decides which questions apply), and so does STARA (classify, then extract named fields).
- Akoma Ntoso separates changes to the words (textual) from changes to meaning, scope, force and efficacy without changing words. Colorado tax provisions that limit a rate to stated tax years, or suspend a credit, are of the second kind and need attributes for the period, not a text-change class.

### Gaps
- **The full list of provision types and the arguments of each was not obtained from any source read in this session.** My unverified recollection, which is not a source and must be checked before use, is that Francesconi and Passerini's eleven classes are Repeal, Definition, Delegation, Delegification, Prohibition, Reservation, Insertion, Obligation, Permission, Penalty and Substitution; that the dataset was 582 provisions; and that typical arguments are of the form Obligation (addressee, action, third party), Definition (definiendum, definiens), Repeal (rule, position, novellato), Insertion and Substitution (rule, position, novella, novellato). The search engine did not confirm the class list, the 582 figure or any accuracy number.
- One secondary source gives "average F1 0.80" for a related 11-type classifier but does not clearly attribute it; not usable.
- To close this gap, allow `disi.unitn.it` (https://disi.unitn.it/~passerini/papers/ailaw07.pdf) or `www.ai.rug.nl` in the session's network settings, or store a copy under `sources/`.
- The meanings of Akoma Ntoso's five non-textual modification types were not read.

## Google LangExtract: classes, attributes, example quality, overlaps, alignment warnings

### Takeaway
LangExtract's schema is whatever the few-shot examples show: a class name, a verbatim span and free-form attributes. Its documentation asks for exact text, order of appearance and no overlapping entities, uses a shared attribute value to group related spans, and warns when an example's own spans do not align exactly with the example's text.

### Cited Findings
- **[read]** An extraction is `extraction_class`, `extraction_text` and `attributes` (a dictionary). The library "enforces a consistent output schema based on your few-shot examples, leveraging controlled generation in supported models". — [LangExtract README](https://github.com/google/langextract/blob/main/README.md)
- **[read]** The README's model prompt: "Extract characters, emotions, and relationships in order of appearance. Use exact text for extractions. Do not paraphrase or overlap entities. Provide meaningful attributes for each entity to add context." — [LangExtract README](https://github.com/google/langextract/blob/main/README.md)
- **[read]** On examples: "Examples drive model behavior. Each `extraction_text` should ideally be verbatim from the example's `text` (no paraphrasing), listed in order of appearance. LangExtract raises `Prompt alignment` warnings by default if examples don't follow this pattern—resolve these for best results." — [LangExtract README](https://github.com/google/langextract/blob/main/README.md)
- **[read]** The check behind that warning (`prompt_validation.py`): each example's extractions are aligned against the example's own text. Levels are `OFF`, `WARNING`, `ERROR`. Issue kinds are `FAILED` (no alignment at all) and `NON_EXACT` (`MATCH_FUZZY` or `MATCH_LESSER`). Fuzzy alignment has a minimum threshold of 0.75. In `ERROR` mode a failure raises `PromptAlignmentError`, and `strict_non_exact` makes non-exact matches errors too. — [langextract/prompt_validation.py](https://github.com/google/langextract/blob/main/langextract/prompt_validation.py)
- **[read]** Grounding failure mode: "LLMs may occasionally extract content from few-shot examples rather than the input text." Such extractions have `char_interval = None` and should be filtered out. — [LangExtract README](https://github.com/google/langextract/blob/main/README.md)
- **[read]** Relations are expressed through attributes, not nesting. The medication example's prompt: "Each entity must have a 'medication_group' attribute linking it to its medication" and "All details about a medication should share the same medication_group value"; classes there are medication, dosage, route, frequency, duration, extracted "in the order they appear in the text". — [LangExtract medication examples](https://github.com/google/langextract/blob/main/docs/examples/medication_examples.md)
- **[read]** Closed attribute values: `output_schema` can restrict an attribute to an enum (the documented example restricts `status` to `present` or `absent`, for "Patient has hypertension. Patient denies diabetes."). Gemini and OpenAI support it; Ollama does not. "LangExtract usually derives provider schema constraints from examples"; with `output_schema`, examples "still guide the prompt". Several classes are combined with `anyOf`. — [LangExtract output_schema example](https://github.com/google/langextract/blob/main/docs/examples/output_schema.md)
- **[read]** Inferred versus textual content is a prompt decision: the README notes its example "stay[s] close to the text evidence" and that a task "could be modified to generate attributes that draw more heavily from the LLM's world knowledge"; accuracy of inferred information depends on the model, the task and "the clarity of the prompt instructions, and the nature of the prompt examples". — [LangExtract README](https://github.com/google/langextract/blob/main/README.md)
- **[read]** Long inputs: chunking, parallel processing and several passes; the long-text example uses `extraction_passes=3` ("Improves recall through multiple passes") and `max_char_buffer=1000` ("Smaller contexts for better accuracy"). — [LangExtract README](https://github.com/google/langextract/blob/main/README.md)
- **[repo notes]** With more than one pass, results are merged and "first extraction wins for overlaps": passes raise recall and are not a vote. Each extraction returns `char_interval` and an `alignment_status` of `match_exact`, `match_greater`, `match_lesser` or `match_fuzzy`. `lx.extract` accepts `additional_context`. — `docs/research/notes/stara_hsu_followup.md` in this repository, recording a read of [langextract/extraction.py](https://github.com/google/langextract/blob/main/langextract/extraction.py)

### Inferences
- Because the examples are the schema, the dictionary's positive examples should be written once and used both as annotator guidance and as LangExtract `ExampleData`; any example span that is not word-for-word in its example text will trip the alignment warning. Running validation at `ERROR` level with `strict_non_exact` gives a mechanical check of the dictionary's own examples.
- "Do not overlap entities" plus "first extraction wins for overlaps" means nested spans (an amount inside a condition) are poorly supported. A dictionary for this tool should prefer non-overlapping spans and express containment through a shared group attribute.
- `alignment_status` is a free per-span quality signal: anything other than `match_exact` is a boundary or wording deviation to review.

### Gaps
- LangExtract documents no guidance on label-set size, negative examples or a "none" class. Negative examples cannot be expressed in `ExampleData` except as an example text with no extraction for that class.
- I did not read the tokenizer or resolver source, so how overlap is detected (character or token level) is not confirmed.

## STARA and "Benchmarking Legal RAG": fields per provision and ambiguities that caused errors

### Takeaway
STARA classifies each small statutory unit and then extracts named fields with reasoning (for federal crimes: offense description and penalty); extraction quality was far below classification quality even in the full system (0.76 against 0.98 precision). The benchmark paper traces many "reasoning errors" to the question, not the model: prompts that did not state the time window, whether expired-but-codified provisions count, or whether listed exceptions control.

### Cited Findings
- **[read]** STARA produces "statutory surveys": "compilations of all provisions relevant to a particular legal issue, together with detailed annotations and reasoning". On federal criminal statutes it found 1,983 provisions with precision 0.98 and recall 0.998 (Gemini Deep Research: 282 found, 0.890 / 0.144; OpenAI Deep Research: 84, 0.881 / 0.044; Westlaw AI Jurisdictional Survey: 113, 0.415 / 0.072). — [STARA project page](https://reglab.github.io/stara)
- **[read]** Ablation (precision / recall / extraction): LLM baseline 0.58 / 0.990 / 0.28; + prompt engineering 0.76 / 0.987 / 0.32; + basic context 0.96 / 0.984 / 0.70; full STARA 0.98 / 0.998 / 0.76 (the page's ablation row prints 0.96 for STARA precision while its comparison table prints 0.98; the repository's earlier note records 0.98 from the paper's Table 4). "Extraction measures accuracy and completeness in identifying offense descriptions and penalties." — [STARA project page](https://reglab.github.io/stara)
- **[read]** Fields: "every provision in our dataset is annotated with rich metadata, including offense descriptions, penalties, and detailed explanations of the legal reasoning behind the classification". — [STARA FAQ](https://reglab.github.io/stara/faq.html)
- **[read]** Why context is needed: "In many cases, individual subsections are nothing more than sentence fragments, understandable only with reference to language contained in a parent provision." STARA "supplies LLMs with the nonlocal context necessary—definitions, cross-references, headings, etc." Its method is "batch annotation followed by aggregation". Westlaw's tool "hallucinated 121 non-existent statutory provisions". — [STARA FAQ](https://reglab.github.io/stara/faq.html)
- **[repo notes]** Unit and context: the unit is the smallest enumerated provision; units under 100 words "often lack sufficient context" and those over 2,000 words "tend to be too complex", so units are merged or split toward about 1,000 words. Each unit gets a heading (citation, position, parent titles), scoped definitions of terms it uses, lead-ins ("chapeaus") and suffixes (provisos in following units). The user's description of relevance is turned into a detailed prompt with multi-step criteria and positive and negative examples; a model then classifies each shortlisted unit, gives reasoning and extracts named fields. Model: Llama 3.3 70B. — `docs/research/notes/stara_hsu_followup.md` in this repository, recording a read of the [STARA paper](https://law.stanford.edu/wp-content/uploads/2025/08/STARA-Daniel-Ho.pdf)
- **[read]** LaborBench: 1,647 binary questions on unemployment insurance law, 40 question types in 8 batches of 5, built from a US Department of Labor compilation. Westlaw AI F1 64%, Lexis+ AI 41%. Apparent false positives: STARA 181, Lexis+ AI 97, Westlaw AI 596. — [Benchmarking Legal RAG, arXiv 2603.03300](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.03300)
- **[read]** "135 out of STARA's 181 apparent false positives actually reflect correct identifications" missing from the expert compilation (the paper rounds this to 75%); 44 were tagged reasoning errors and 2 system errors. "Many of the 44 entries tagged as reasoning errors arise from how the benchmark treats older but still codified provisions. A statute can remain in the code with historical effect only, and the prompt does not specify whether such expired provisions" count. Examples given are Michigan and Indiana weekly-benefit methods retained in the code as historical rules. — [Benchmarking Legal RAG, §4.3.1](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.03300)
- **[read]** Design principle 5.1.1, quoted: "Well-designed survey prompts should state the evaluation window, specify whether historical provisions still codified count as positive findings, and clarify whether enumerated exception conditions control outcomes. Without these specifications, identical statutory texts may yield contradictory interpretations across systems." — [Benchmarking Legal RAG, §5.1.1](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.03300)
- **[read]** Principle 5.1.2: questions about "differences from federal definitions" must say "whether any deviation counts or only substantive changes to core requirements matter"; the "employer" definition example has identical monetary thresholds across states but different exemptions. — [Benchmarking Legal RAG, §5.1.2](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.03300)
- **[read]** Other error patterns: a Michigan false positive came from reading an information-sharing provision as deduction authority; false negatives clustered in two questions where provisions "using different phrasing were sometimes excluded before classification" by the keyword filter; the paper's stated limit is that binary True/False labels "reduc[e] complex legal interpretation to a single outcome". — [Benchmarking Legal RAG](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.03300)
- **[repo notes]** 69 false negatives, some because the rule sat in a regulation or agency interpretation and STARA searched statutes only; corrected accuracy 92%, up from 83%. — `docs/research/notes/stara_hsu_followup.md` in this repository

### Inferences
- Three scope decisions belong in every class definition, since leaving them out produced measurable error: the time window (does a provision limited to past tax years count?), the treatment of exceptions (is the exception part of the span, a separate span, or an attribute?), and what counts as a difference worth marking.
- Expert hand marks are a reference, not truth: three quarters of the model's "errors" against an attorney compilation were the attorneys' omissions. Model-only spans need adjudication with a "reference was wrong" outcome.
- Field extraction is the weak step even with full context (0.76), so the dictionary's quality checks should concentrate on spans and values, not on provision typing.

### Gaps
- The STARA paper PDF could not be fetched in this session (TLS failure on `dho.stanford.edu`), so the exact field list per task, the prompt wording and how "extraction" was scored were not re-read. The paper is not on arXiv as far as I could find.
- No STARA prompts, schema or code are published (per the repository's earlier note on the `reglab/stara` repositories).

## General annotation-guideline practice: label granularity, span rules, nesting, "none" policy, adjudication, versioning, agreement figures

### Takeaway
The best-documented legal span projects keep the label set small or fix the span unit, write an explicit "none" class, give annotators an "unsure" escape, adjudicate with a single expert, and report agreement with a span-aware measure. Reported figures range from Krippendorff's alpha 0.89 on a three-label statute task to exact-match annotator F1 of about 0.3-0.54 on a ten-label case-law task. Kappa for span boundaries in legal text was not found.

### Cited Findings
- **[read]** Annotares (German statutes, 2026): three labels only, Tatbestand (legal conditions), Rechtsfolge (legal consequence) and none ("the 'outside' class"). More than 400 sentences from the Federal Data Protection Act plus 50 each from two other codes as out-of-domain tests; more than 21,500 tokens, about 43% conditions, 44% consequences, 13% none. — [Annotares, arXiv 2608.03898](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.03898)
- **[read]** Annotares guideline design: rules are based on "identifiable linguistic patterns" so that "even non-domain experts can perform the task in a uniform, reproducible manner". A structural constraint ties labels together: "if a sentence contains a Tatbestand (legal conditions), it must also contain a Rechtsfolge (legal consequence), and vice versa"; sentences without that structure are labeled entirely none. The guideline examples cover a linear case, an inverted order, "multiple, interleaved spans of both conditions and consequences", and a none case. — [Annotares](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.03898)
- **[read]** Annotares cue words and a tie-break rule: conditions are often introduced by wenn, falls, sofern but "are not always introduced by explicit conjunctions"; consequences often carry modal verbs kann (discretionary), soll (guided discretion), muss (mandatory). For actors: "When an actor is part of the situational prerequisite, they are labeled as Tatbestand. If the actor is primarily the subject or object of the resulting legal action, they are included in the Rechtsfolge span." — [Annotares](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.03898)
- **[read]** Annotares agreement and adjudication: six annotators; the lead author annotated everything and every sentence had at least two independent annotations. Token-level Krippendorff's alpha 0.893 (BDSG, six annotators), 0.897 (BAföG), 0.926 (BauGB); pairwise expert-annotator values from 0.826 to 0.942. Annotators had an "unsure" flag; flagged sentences were excluded from the agreement figure and the dataset (seven removed). In adjudication, 84.4% of cases were identical or fixed by "deterministic corrections of trivial errors (e.g., adjusting misplaced punctuation at span boundaries)"; the rest went to the expert in 12.7% and to the other annotator in 2.9%. — [Annotares](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.03898)
- **[read]** CuRIAM (US Supreme Court opinions): ten categories in a flat schema that "can be thought of as falling into three broad groups"; categories "are allowed to overlap" (a Focal Term inside a Direct Quote). Each category has a one-line definition, e.g. "Definition: Succinct, reasonably self-contained description of what a word or phrase means"; "Legal Source: Citation or mention appealing to a legal document or authority". 59,693 tokens, 6,088 annotated instances. Categories were refined with four law-student annotators over several pilot rounds; five opinions were annotated by all four and discussed, then each of 27 opinions went to two annotators. — [CuRIAM, arXiv 2305.14719](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.14719)
- **[read]** CuRIAM agreement: unlabeled exact-match F1 per annotator 0.540, 0.542, 0.483 and 0.298; the lowest is the annotator "who received the least training". Exact-match F1 by category for the first three annotators: Language Source 0.679-0.824, Direct Quote 0.478-0.624, Legal Source 0.547-0.604, Focal Term 0.356-0.451, Metalinguistic Cue 0.320-0.405, Definition 0.240-0.295, Example Use 0.118-0.146, Named Interpretive Rule 0.091-0.174. — [CuRIAM, Tables 4-5](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.14719)
- **[read]** CuRIAM on matching rules: exact match needs "the exact same span"; partial match counts "any overlap between two spans" with the same category. Partial matching shows "how annotators approach longer spans and how features like punctuation impact agreement". Rare categories with broad definitions had low or mixed agreement; "Appeal to Meaning" had high agreement "especially when allowing partial matching" but annotators "still reported difficulty ... due to the broad definition". Short spans were "easy to agree on when pointed out" but "easier to miss"; the authors suggest heuristic pre-annotation for such categories. — [CuRIAM](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.14719)
- **[read]** Label count against agreement, as reported by LegalBench for models: nearly all models struggled on two tasks that "require the LLM to classify text into one of six or more categories". — [LegalBench](https://arxiv.org/abs/2308.11462)
- **[read]** Minimal versus maximal spans, as practised: CUAD marks "every clause ... most relevant" and accepts that "only a sub-part of a sentence" may be responsive (sub-sentence spans allowed); ContractNLI fixes sentence or list-item units and asks for the fewest units that are self-contained; MAUD's abridged set drops irrelevant text inside a span and marks the gap with `<omitted>`. — [CUAD](https://arxiv.org/abs/2103.06268); [ContractNLI](https://arxiv.org/abs/2110.01799); [MAUD](https://arxiv.org/abs/2301.00876)
- **[read]** "None of the above" as practised: CUAD and SQuAD 2.0 use an empty answer per label; ContractNLI has NotMentioned with no evidence; Annotares has an explicit none label and reports exact-span scores for it "as a proxy for how well a model internalized the underlying structural rules". — [CUAD](https://arxiv.org/abs/2103.06268); [ContractNLI](https://arxiv.org/abs/2110.01799); [Annotares](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.03898)
- **[read]** Adjudication models seen: single expert adjudicator (Annotares, ContractNLI's primary annotator); team consensus then lawyer escalation then full lawyer review (MAUD); student flagging then attorney consensus plus a machine-suggested recall pass (CUAD). — [Annotares](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.03898); [ContractNLI](https://arxiv.org/abs/2110.01799); [MAUD](https://arxiv.org/abs/2301.00876); [CUAD](https://arxiv.org/abs/2103.06268)
- **[read]** Missing agreement records are common even in large expert projects: MAUD kept none and reports only that lawyers "agreed about 80% of the time" with students; CUAD reports none. — [MAUD](https://arxiv.org/abs/2301.00876); [CUAD](https://arxiv.org/abs/2103.06268)

### Inferences
- Agreement falls as the label set grows and as definitions get broader: three structural labels reach alpha 0.89; ten interpretive labels reach exact-match F1 near 0.5 at best, with the rarest and broadest classes lowest. A tax dictionary should keep few top-level classes and push detail into attributes.
- Punctuation at span edges is the single most common harmless disagreement (Annotares: most of the 84.4% resolved "deterministically"). A boundary rule on leading and trailing punctuation, articles and connectives ("and", "or", "if") removes it before it happens.
- Report two agreement figures, exact match and overlap match, per class. The gap between them separates boundary disagreement from disagreement about whether the thing is there at all.
- An "unsure" flag with exclusion, a cross-label constraint (a condition needs a consequence), and a linguistic-cue list are three cheap devices that a statute dictionary can adopt as quality checks.

### Gaps
- No source read here reports Cohen's or Fleiss' kappa for span boundaries in legal text; the search found none either. Token-level alpha and annotator F1 are what is published.
- I found nothing in these sources on guideline versioning (numbered guideline releases, re-annotation after a rule change). CuRIAM mentions "several rounds of pilot annotation" and CUAD "over 100 pages of rules", but neither describes version control.
- No controlled study of label-set size against agreement in legal text was found; the relation above is read across different projects, which differ in many other ways.
- CuRIAM's partial-match table (Table 6) values were not extracted.

## How consistently LLMs reproduce span boundaries, and which schema choices help

### Takeaway
Boundaries are where models fail: a model can label most tokens correctly and still get few or no spans exactly right. The evidence found supports fixing the unit in advance, pairing a verbatim span with a normalised or enumerated attribute, checking every span against the source text, and keeping the prompt a plain statement of the guidelines.

### Cited Findings
- **[read]** Annotares: "A span is only considered correct if its boundaries match the gold standard exactly without gaps or shifts." A BiLSTM-CRF reached 0.772 token accuracy on consequences "but completely failed to predict a single exact span" for either class (0.000). "Even if a model correctly identifies the core of a legal condition, failing to precisely identify the boundary results in a total span failure." Token accuracy "should not be used as the only metric for this task". — [Annotares](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.03898)
- **[read]** Annotares, LLM results: Claude Haiku and Opus with "Prompt 1", described as "a textual representation of the annotation guidelines, including class definitions, mandatory structural rules, and few-shot examples", were competitive with domain-specific BERT models (best overall was mBERT, accuracy 85.4%, macro-F1 0.798). A more elaborate "Prompt 2" with dependency tags "yielded lower mF1 scores", which the authors read as: "increasing prompt complexity may introduce noise that detracts from the model's instruction-following capabilities". Opus with Prompt 2 had the highest exact-span match on the none class (0.875) despite lower token accuracy. Most systems "struggled to detect or enforce the underlying logical rule of the corpus" (none cannot co-occur with the other two labels in one normative sentence). — [Annotares](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.03898)
- **[read]** Models favoured the majority class: "most models favor the Rechtsfolge (legal consequence) over the other classes", with consequences identified best, then conditions, then none. — [Annotares](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.03898)
- **[search]** A 2025 study of GPT-4 and other models on legal term extraction separated perfect from partial matches and reported 270 exact matches out of 973 legal terms for GPT-4; the summary says zero-shot models trade flexibility for weaker boundary precision. Not read; figures unverified. — [Leveraging LLMs for legal terms extraction with limited annotated data, Artificial Intelligence and Law](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10506-025-09448-8)
- **[search]** A clinical named-entity study reports micro-F1 rising from about 0.67 under exact matching to about 0.72 under partial matching. Not legal text, not read. — [Span-based annotation framework for LLM-based clinical NER](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12657458/)
- **[read]** Fixing the unit removes the boundary problem: ContractNLI defines spans in advance and has the model classify them, because predicting start and end tokens forces "span boundary detection and evidence identification concurrently". — [ContractNLI](https://arxiv.org/abs/2110.01799)
- **[read]** Verbatim span plus closed-value attribute: LangExtract keeps `extraction_text` verbatim and puts interpretation in attributes, which can be enum-constrained through `output_schema`; CUAD pairs each span with a yes/no or normalised answer; MAUD pairs each extracted text with answers from a predefined list. — [LangExtract output_schema example](https://github.com/google/langextract/blob/main/docs/examples/output_schema.md); [CUAD](https://arxiv.org/abs/2103.06268); [MAUD](https://arxiv.org/abs/2301.00876)
- **[read]** Mechanical grounding: LangExtract aligns every extraction to source characters and reports spans it cannot locate (`char_interval = None`), including text copied from the few-shot examples. — [LangExtract README](https://github.com/google/langextract/blob/main/README.md)
- **[read]** Context raises extraction quality more than prompt wording: in STARA's ablation, extraction went from 0.28 (baseline) to 0.32 with prompt engineering and to 0.70 once basic context was added. — [STARA project page](https://reglab.github.io/stara)
- **[read]** Scoring generated extractions needs normalisation "to account for differences in tense/casing/punctuation", which is an admission that models do not return text character for character. — [LegalBench](https://arxiv.org/abs/2308.11462)
- **[read]** Required-slot design precedent: SARA's slots, MAUD's type-determined question sets and Annotares' paired-label constraint all make some elements mandatory once a type is assigned. — [SARA](https://arxiv.org/abs/2005.05257); [MAUD](https://arxiv.org/abs/2301.00876); [Annotares](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.03898)

### Inferences
- Score spans three ways, exact, overlap and normalised-value match, and treat exact match as the strict check and the value as what the rules engine actually consumes. A span with the right value and a boundary off by a comma should not count as a wrong parameter.
- Where the engine needs a number or a date, make the normalised attribute the required element and the verbatim span its evidence; this matches the project rule that spans must appear word for word while values feed computation.
- Write deterministic boundary rules (no leading or trailing punctuation; include the currency sign and the unit; whole number phrases) so that most boundary differences can be repaired by a script, as Annotares did in adjudication.
- Use the annotation guideline itself as the prompt, with its definitions, structural rules and examples; the one comparison found suggests that elaborating beyond that hurt.
- Enforce cross-class constraints in code after extraction (a rate needs the base it applies to; a condition needs a consequence), since models did not reliably honour such a rule from the prompt alone.

### Gaps
- I found no study that measures run-to-run consistency of span boundaries for the same model and prompt (self-agreement), and none that isolates the effect of constrained decoding or required-slot schemas on span accuracy in legal text. The schema recommendations above are inferences from practice, not measured results.
- The two search-only findings (270 of 973; 0.67 to 0.72) were not verified against the papers.
- Annotares' per-model exact-span table could not be mapped to model names reliably from the converted text, so only figures stated in its prose are given.
