      Placed on the ballot by citizen initiative • Passes with a majority vote


 1    Amendment 87 proposes amending the Colorado Constitution and
 2    Colorado statutes to:
 3    ●   remove the constitutional requirement that the state income tax be set at a single flat rate;
 4    ●   replace the current flat income tax with a graduated state income tax that sets lower rates

 6    ●   spend the additional revenue collected from these income tax rate changes on K-12

 8    ●   exempt additional revenue collected under the measure from the state’s constitutional

24     Summary and Analysis of Amendment 87
25    What does Amendment 87 do?
26    The measure replaces the state’s current flat income tax rate for individuals and businesses with
27    a graduated income tax that lowers taxes for taxable income below $500,000 and raises taxes for
28    taxable income above $500,000. It requires that the additional money collected from this change
29    be spent on K-12 education, health care, and early childhood care and education, and exempts
30    this money from the state’s constitutional revenue limit, also known as the Taxpayer’s Bill of
31    Rights (TABOR) limit. The state must produce an audited annual report on how much additional
32    revenue was collected and how it was spent.

33   What is the state’s current income tax structure?
34   Since 1987, Colorado has had a flat income tax rate, in which all taxpayers, including individuals
35   and businesses, pay the same income tax rate on their income. The state’s current flat income
36   tax rate is 4.4 percent. The majority of state income tax revenue is used for general state
37   programs and services. The TABOR amendment in the state constitution requires that income be
38   taxed at a flat rate.
39   Income tax is paid on a portion of a household’s or business’s income, which is called taxable
40   income. For example, a married couple earning $42,000 in income per year may have about
41   $10,000 in taxable income and owe $440 in taxes annually. A married couple earning $130,000
42   in income per year may have about $100,000 in taxable income and owe $4,400 in taxes
43   annually.
44   What is the graduated income tax structure proposed in Amendment 87?
45   Amendment 87 removes the constitutional requirement that income be taxed at a flat rate and
46   creates a graduated income tax starting in 2027. In this graduated tax system, an individual’s or
47   business’s income is split into six ranges—called brackets—with the lowest income tax bracket
48   taxed at the lowest rate and each higher bracket taxed at increasing rates, as shown in Table 1.
49   This is similar to how the federal income tax works. Unlike the federal income tax, the new state
50   income tax brackets are not adjusted for inflation, so more of taxpayers’ incomes will fall into
51   the higher brackets over time. Each tax rate is only applied to the portion of taxable income that
52   falls into that bracket.

63   How does Amendment 87 change the amount of taxes owed?
64   For individuals and businesses with taxable incomes of less than $500,000, the measure
65   decreases the amount of income taxes owed, up to a maximum reduction of $325 per taxpayer.
66   For taxpayers with incomes of about $500,000 and above, the measure increases the amount of
67   income taxes owed.

 72   How does Amendment 87 change state revenue?
 73   By changing tax rates for individuals and businesses, Amendment 87 is expected to increase
 74   state income tax revenue by an estimated $2 billion in the first full year and more in later years.
 75   However, actual revenue collections may be more or less than this estimate depending on
 76   economic conditions and other factors.
 77   The change in state revenue under the measure is calculated as the difference between the
 78   actual income tax revenue collected under the measure’s graduated income tax rates and the
 79   income tax revenue that would have been collected under the state’s current rate of 4.4 percent.
 80   See the Fiscal Impact section below for additional detail.
 81   Does Amendment 87 impact TABOR refunds?
 82   The measure has no impact on current TABOR refunds or TABOR refund mechanisms. Under
 83   current law, the state is expected to collect $15 billion in income tax revenue in 2028. Under this
 84   measure, it is expected to collect $2 billion more in income tax revenue, for a total of $17 billion.
 85   The additional $2 billion is exempt from the TABOR limit. The TABOR limit and the amount of
 86   revenue subject to the limit remain the same, and estimated TABOR refunds will not change. For
 87   additional information, see the LCS memorandum titled, “A Brief Overview of the TABOR
 88   Revenue Limit.”1
 89   How will the additional revenue be spent?
 90   The increased revenue collected under Amendment 87 must be spent on K-12 education, health
 91   care, and early childhood care and education. It must add to, and not replace, spending on
 92   those services. The state legislature will decide what specific programs will receive additional
 93   funding and how much.
 94   What happens if Amendment 87 and Proposition 136 both pass?
 95   Proposition 136 limits the state income tax rate at the current law rate of 4.4 percent. If both
 96   Amendment 87 and Proposition 136 are approved by voters, the proposed tax rates for income
 97   over $500,000 in Amendment 87 would be higher than the 4.4 percent limit in Proposition 136.
 98   Generally, when parts of voter-approved measures conflict, the policy outcome is determined by
 99   the measure that received more votes. However, if both measures are approved by voters, the
100   exact outcome is unclear. The state legislature or a court will need to determine how to resolve
101   any conflicts.


102   Arguments For Amendment 87
103   1) Amendment 87 cuts taxes for 97 percent of taxpayers, including most households and small
104      businesses, while increasing taxes only for top earners who have the ability to pay. A
105      graduated income tax is more equitable than a flat tax because it recognizes a fundamental
106      economic reality: each dollar taxed on someone earning $30,000 imposes a far greater

107      hardship than each dollar taxed on someone earning $1 million. By aligning tax rates with
108      taxpayers’ financial means, a graduated tax distributes the burden more justly across all
109      income levels, lowers taxes for most households and small businesses, and puts more money
110      in the hands of working families who will spend it in their local communities.
111   2) The measure funds critical services at a time when additional funding is desperately needed.
112      Crucial programs are at risk of further cuts because of changes in federal policy. Revenue
113      from the graduated tax will fund education, health care, and child care, public services that
114      many Colorado households rely on. Colorado public schools are significantly underfunded,
115      and constraints on the state budget make Medicaid cuts increasingly likely. Additional
116      funding will provide more security for the people who depend on these services.
117   3) The graduated income tax is not an experiment, but a proven approach for funding essential
118      public services. The federal government and 27 other states already have a graduated
119      income tax. This tax structure lessens rising wealth inequality and increases funding for state
120      programs. Sales and property taxes place greater burdens on lower-income households, and
121      a graduated income tax corrects for this inequity while also funding services for all
122      Coloradans.


123   Arguments Against Amendment 87
124   1) The measure is a multibillion-dollar tax increase, and hiking taxes on higher incomes will
125      harm Colorado’s economy. Higher tax rates make Colorado a less competitive place to start
126      and grow a business, and may encourage some high earners to leave the state entirely.
127      When businesses and high-earning individuals relocate, people at all income levels may lose
128      their jobs. Further, because the brackets do not increase with inflation, more and more
129      taxpayers will pay these higher taxes each year. Colorado cannot risk employers and jobs
130      fleeing the state because our taxes are too high. Keeping taxes competitive for everyone
131      fuels economic activity and builds a stronger, more prosperous Colorado for all.
132   2) The measure is an attack on the fair and predictable tax system that TABOR currently
133      guarantees. The state’s current flat tax is fair because everyone has equal access to state
134      services, high-income earners already pay more taxes, and low-income earners qualify for
135      more tax deductions and credits. The state should better prioritize the tens of billions of
136      dollars it already collects, rather than switch to a new, complicated tax system.
137   3) The measure will permanently expand the size of government, increase careless and wasteful
138      spending of taxpayer dollars, and hinder economic growth. Instead of spending billions of
139      dollars more on broadly defined government services, state policy should focus on lowering
140      taxes for everyone or funding specific programs that are proven to help Colorado’s
141      residents.

163   State Revenue
164   By replacing the state flat income tax with a graduated income tax, the measure increases
165   revenue from income taxes by an estimated $958.4 million in budget year 2026-27, $1.972
166   billion in budget year 2027-28, and increasing amounts in future years based on income and
167   population growth. The estimate for budget year 2026-27 represents a half-year impact for tax
168   year 2027. The additional revenue will be credited to the Colorado Future’s Account created in
169   the measure. The increased revenue is exempt from TABOR as a voter-approved revenue
170   change. The state budget year begins each July 1 and ends on June 30.
171   State law requires Legislative Council Staff to include an estimate of the maximum dollar amount
172   of the change in state government revenue for the first full fiscal year of a proposed tax
173   increase. This is the number that appears in the ballot question. The estimates in the previous
174   paragraph represent the revenue impact of the measure under the current Legislative Council
175   Staff revenue forecast. Based on forecast error that could occur, the maximum dollar amount of
176   the change in state government revenue and fiscal year spending for budget year 2027-28 is
177   estimated as an increase of $2.7 billion.
178   State Spending
179   The measure increases the amount of state revenue available to spend for the purposes
180   identified in the measure, including K-12 education, health care, and early childhood care and
181   education, in FY 2026-27 and future fiscal years. To administer the tax rate change, the measure
182   is expected to increase one-time spending in the Department of Revenue by $98,000.
183   School District Revenue and Spending
184   The measure increases available state funding to school districts. The exact amount distributed
185   to school districts will depend on decisions made by the General Assembly when allocating
186   money from the Colorado Future’s Account.

187   State Spending and Tax Increases
188   Article X, Section 20, of the Colorado Constitution requires that the following fiscal information
189   be provided when a tax increase question is on the ballot:
190   ●   estimates or actual amounts of state fiscal year spending for the current year and each of the
191       past four years with the overall percentage and dollar change; and
192   ●   for the first full year of the proposed tax increase, estimates of the maximum dollar amount
193       of the tax increase and of fiscal year spending without the increase.
194   “Fiscal year spending” is a legal term in the Colorado Constitution. It equals the amount of
195   revenue subject to the constitutional spending limit that the state or a district is permitted to
196   keep and either spend or save for a single year. Table 4 shows state fiscal year spending for the
