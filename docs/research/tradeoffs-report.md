# Amendment 87 reorders Colorado's tax-as-code roadmap

Colorado can turn its tax law into a machine-readable public good, but the four layers trade off differently, and the best first move is the cheapest one: publish parameters and test cases, contribute income tax rules to the existing open model, and leave structured statute text and sales tax logic to the official publishers. The legal barriers are low. The state stopped copyrighting statute annotations and charging for its statutory database in 2016, and it has already legislated a reliance safe harbour for machine-readable sales tax data. The real constraints are format (statutes in SGML on request, rules in PDF), authority (no unofficial copy or code base carries evidentiary weight or audit protection) and stewardship (agency-only projects die politically, nonprofit-only projects die financially). An open Colorado income tax module already exists in PolicyEngine, so building a new engine would duplicate the federal base that Colorado law piggybacks on. Sales tax is a different problem: roughly 66 self-collecting home-rule cities control their own bases, so it is a data-publication and liability question, not an engine question. The November 2026 vote sharpens the sequencing. Amendment 87, reported passed with about 51.4% but not yet certified, replaces the flat 4.4% rate with six brackets for tax years beginning 1 January 2027, which makes a dated, provenance-tagged bracket table and a tax year 2027 test corpus the most valuable near-term artefacts. Several load-bearing facts remain unverified, including the measure's filing-status and indexing rules, its interaction with TABOR rate cuts, and whether the sales tax task force survived its 1 July 2026 repeal date; the last section lists them.

## Structured legal text is legally open but technically locked

**Copyright is not the obstacle.** The Committee on Legal Services stopped copyrighting editorial material ancillary to the Colorado Revised Statutes and suspended fees for the statutory database on 4 March 2016; before that the state earned only **$16,000 a year** from three vendors ([Colorado Revised Statutes Data page](http://content.leg.colorado.gov/agencies/office-legislative-legal-services/colorado-revised-statutes-data); [Colorado Freedom of Information Coalition](https://coloradofoic.org/colorado-statutes-database-should-be-free-lawmakers-decide/)). The Supreme Court's 2020 decision in Georgia v. Public.Resource.Org then held that annotations authored by an arm of the legislature are not copyrightable ([Supreme Court opinion](https://www.supremecourt.gov/opinions/19pdf/590us1r30_3f14.pdf)). Colorado's annotations are prepared by the Office of Legislative Legal Services, so they sit in the same position. Two caveats matter to an insider. First, the 2016 action was a committee suspension, and C.R.S. 2-5-118 still contains fee and copyright hooks and requires a written application to republish ancillary material, so the openness rests on policy that a future committee could reverse ([C.R.S. 2-5-118, unofficial mirror](https://colorado.public.law/statutes/crs_2-5-118)). Second, a 2021 Colorado Law clinic brief asserted that the LexisNexis publishing contract still incorrectly claimed copyright and blocked downloading from the hosted site; the brief itself and the current contract were not read, so that claim rests on the clinic's summary ([TLPC, December 2021](https://tlpc.colorado.edu/tlpc-releases-policy-brief-on-access-to-colorado-law-and-the-government-edicts-doctrine/)).

**Format and process are the obstacle.** The statutory database is "available only as text files in SGML format", delivered as a zip through a request form, with no stated version history; the title-by-title DOCX, PDF and HTM downloads are labelled for personal use ([Colorado Revised Statutes Data page](http://content.leg.colorado.gov/agencies/office-legislative-legal-services/colorado-revised-statutes-data); [C.R.S. page](https://content.leg.colorado.gov/agencies/office-legislative-legal-services/colorado-revised-statutes)). The clinic brief urged the state to finish an in-house XML project, and the data page still says SGML only, which suggests that project has not shipped. The Code of Colorado Regulations is the mirror image: the Secretary of State publishes per-rule versions back to 2007, each with a digital certificate since 31 March 2014, but only as PDFs, with no bulk download or API found ([SOS CCR FAQs](https://coloradosos.gov/pubs/CCR/FAQs.html)). The statutes therefore have structure without history or authentication, and the tax rules in 1 CCR 201 have history and authentication without structure. A unified Title 39 plus 1 CCR 201 dataset spans two official publishers, and neither can authenticate the whole.

**The schema choice matters less than it looks.** Because every Colorado source needs a custom parser, the marginal cost of emitting one format over another from a parsed tree is small next to the cost of parsing and annual re-validation. The table summarises the tradeoff; the JSON and Markdown row is analysis only, since no sourced evaluation of such projects was retrieved, and no US state was identified as publishing in either USLM or Akoma Ntoso.

| Format | Case for | Case against |
|---|---|---|
| USLM | Built for US code hierarchy; actively maintained by GPO with a no-breaking-change policy; the Internal Revenue Code that Title 39 references is natively USLM ([usgpo/uslm](https://github.com/usgpo/uslm)) | Governed by federal legislative bodies with no state seat; no state adopter found; point-in-time semantics left to the publisher |
| Akoma Ntoso | International OASIS standard with a richer model for lifecycle and versions; used by several parliaments | A 2021 paper calls its adoption "strikingly low" and blames conversion labour and scarce XML-literate lawyers ([arXiv 2108.08678](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2108.08678)) |
| Custom XML in git | Proven by the DC Council, whose law-xml repository shows 834 commits ([DCCouncil/law-xml](https://github.com/DCCouncil/law-xml)) | Bespoke schema, bespoke tooling |
| JSON or Markdown in git | Lowest barrier for developers and for rules-as-code linkage | No standard semantics for amendments or effective dates; no archival or authentication standing |

**Authority is the binding constraint, and only the publisher can lift it.** Under C.R.S. 2-5-118 only the General Assembly's publication is the official statutes and admissible as evidence, and no republisher may claim otherwise. Colorado's enactment of the Uniform Electronic Legal Material Act gives authenticated official material a presumption of accuracy that an unofficial copy never gains ([C.R.S. 24-71.5-106, unofficial mirror](https://colorado.public.law/statutes/crs_24-71.5-106)). Precedent points the same way. The durable systems are those where structured data is the publisher's own working format: the DC Council with the nonprofit Open Law Library, the UK's legislation.gov.uk, the US Code. Even the UK service targets three months to apply amendments and leaves most secondary legislation unrevised ([legislation.gov.uk](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/understanding-legislation)). Third-party republication is fragile: The State Decoded ran on a single **$165,000** Knight grant ([Nieman Lab](https://niemanlab.org/2011/06/the-state-decoded-news-challenge-winner-makes-the-law-more-user-friendly-and-easier-on-the-eyes)). For a builder, that argues for a narrow, clearly unofficial Title 39 conversion with per-section hashes and visible "as of" dates. For an advocate and an insider, it argues that the real prize is getting the Revisor and the Secretary of State to emit structured, authenticated output themselves. Annual statute editions also cap point-in-time granularity at one snapshot a year unless the project applies session laws itself, which replicates the Revisor's work and invites drift.

## Parameters as data: the case is strongest where the state already pays per query

**Colorado sales tax needs three layers, and rates are the easy one.** As of October 2025 the Department of Revenue administered sales taxes for 52 counties, 166 cities and 33 special districts, while **66 home-rule municipalities** collected their own, producing more than 400 unique taxing areas ([CDOR SUTS Functionality Study](https://mtc.gov/wp-content/uploads/2025/11/SUTS-Functionality-Study-CDOR-October-2025.pdf)). Counts differ by source and unit: the Tax Foundation counted 74 self-collectors in 2019, and the department's September 2026 notice says the system covers 600 taxing entities ([Tax Foundation](https://taxfoundation.org/blog/colorado-wayfair-complexity-legal/); [CDOR bulk lookup pause](https://tax.colorado.gov/Bulk-Address-Lookup-Pause)). Any open dataset must define its unit explicitly. Correct tax requires boundaries, rates and per-jurisdiction taxability, and home-rule taxability belongs to each city council. A rates-only file would create false confidence precisely where sellers err, in the base.

**SUTS is a lookup service, not an open dataset, and its metered model has already failed once.** The Sales and Use Tax System pairs a GIS lookup (address-level jurisdictions, rate history, a taxability matrix of over 350 items, a login-gated API) with a filing portal that reaches all but two self-collecting cities; the department itself says it is not a centralised system of record. The free bulk lookup was funded at **$1.275 million a year for 75 million lookups**; fiscal year 2025-26 usage hit **136.5 million**, and the department paused the service on 29 September 2026 with four days' notice, planning a "managed" return on 1 January 2027 ([CDOR bulk lookup pause](https://tax.colorado.gov/Bulk-Address-Lookup-Pause)). That works out to about 1.7 cents per address, against a near-zero marginal cost for a published boundary and rate file. This is the strongest fiscal argument for bulk open data anywhere in the stack. The counterweights are that the state loses usage telemetry and that it is unknown whether the department holds redistribution rights to the GIS layer; the GIS vendor and contract terms were not confirmed.

**Liability runs in opposite directions depending on who publishes.** A vendor that relies on the state GIS database is held harmless in audit for errors in that data, provided it keeps documentation and queried a complete address; the protection reaches home-rule cities only if they participate ([CDOR hold-harmless provision](https://tax.colorado.gov/gis-hold-harmless-provision)). The department's general open-data terms do the reverse: data is "as is", users indemnify the state, and feeds can stop at any time ([CDOR Open Data Use Terms](https://cdor.colorado.gov/department-of-revenue-open-data-use-terms)). A third-party dataset inherits the second regime, never the first. Streamlined Sales Tax member states show the alternative: free downloadable rate and boundary files with liability relief attached ([NC DOR](https://www.ncdor.gov/taxes-forms/sales-and-use-tax/other-sales-and-use-tax-resources/streamlined-sales-tax-information/rate-and-boundary-database-information)). Colorado is not a member ([Streamlined Sales Tax Governing Board](https://www.streamlinedsalestax.org/state-details/colorado)). The policy question is therefore less "open or not" than "will the state publish files and extend the hold-harmless to file-based use". The existing seed is real: rates change on a fixed 1 January and 1 July cycle and the department already issues a combined spreadsheet of rates and location codes ([CDOR rate lookup page](https://tax.colorado.gov/how-to-look-up-sales-use-tax-rates)).

**Income tax parameters are few, conditional and late-binding.** Under pre-2027 law the rate is a flat 4.4% that a TABOR surplus formula can cut retroactively, and credits switch on revenue triggers: the state EITC falls from 50% to 25% of the federal credit for tax year 2026, and the Family Affordability Tax Credit is paused for 2026 ([CDOR CTC and EITC page](https://tax.colorado.gov/CTC-EITC)). No single body publishes a consolidated table, and third-party guides visibly disagree on the 2025 rate and still list paused credit amounts ([TaxAct](https://blog.taxact.com/what-is-colorado-state-income-tax/); [Reed Corp](https://reedcorp.tax/helpful-guides/colorado-state-income-tax/)). An open parameter file with effective dates, a status field (forecast, certified, enacted) and a source for every value would fix a demonstrated problem at low cost. The risk is the same as everywhere else in this report: an unofficial file carries no authority, and Legislative Council Staff itself warns that actual refund amounts "may vary considerably from the forecast" ([Legislative Council Staff memo, August 2026](https://content.leg.colorado.gov/sites/default/files/r26-757-the-tabor-limit-accessible.pdf)).

## Rules as code: an open Colorado model exists, and its limits are specific

**Only one engine has Colorado content.** PolicyEngine's policyengine-us holds the Colorado individual income tax chain plus the state EITC, child tax credit, Family Affordability credit, child care, care worker and senior housing credits, and the TABOR sales tax refund ([GitHub API listing](https://api.github.com/repos/PolicyEngine/policyengine-us/contents/policyengine_us/variables/gov/states/co/tax/income/credits)). A full-tree path search on 11 November 2026 found **no Property Tax/Rent/Heat rebate**, only Denver's separate local program ([GitHub tree](https://api.github.com/repos/PolicyEngine/policyengine-us/git/trees/main?recursive=1)). The subtraction list is short and ends in a catch-all input, so long-tail subtractions and business credits are unmodelled; this is my reading of the directory listing. The model is AGPL-3.0, and there is no transaction-level sales tax model in it. MyFriendBen, the Colorado benefits screener spun out of Gary Community Ventures, shows the production pattern: it calls PolicyEngine's API for the big programs and keeps its own MPL-2.0 Django rules for the rest ([MyFriendBen/benefits-api](https://github.com/MyFriendBen/benefits-api)).

**The alternatives trade maturity for fidelity.** Catala interleaves code with statute text and uses default logic to mirror rule-and-exception drafting, which gives the strongest traceability for a legal audience, but its own README says "the compiler is yet unstable" and it has no US state corpus ([CatalaLang/catala](https://github.com/CatalaLang/catala)). The IRS Fact Graph is designed to reason over incomplete information, which suits an interview product, but the IRS disclaims maintaining or guaranteeing it and its host program is gone: Direct File was suspended in late 2025 and did not run in 2026 ([IRS-Public/fact-graph](https://github.com/IRS-Public/fact-graph); [CRS IF13314](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-17_IF13314_7a5be865820f33882647d91cc0cadc376e538af6.html)). Blawx describes itself as "not production-quality software" ([Lexpedite/blawx](https://www.github.com/Lexpedite/blawx)). Cosilico and the Rules Foundation's `.rac` format, tied to PolicyEngine's founder, promise statute-shaped encoding with AI-assisted validation, but the API is marked "coming soon" and no accuracy figures were found ([cosilico.ai](https://cosilico.ai)). Because Colorado tax starts from federal taxable income, choosing any engine other than PolicyEngine means rebuilding or bridging the federal base.

**Accuracy claims are model-versus-model.** PolicyEngine validates against NBER's TAXSIM with a $15 tolerance and describes its own benchmark outputs as "not administrative truth" ([policyengine-taxsim](https://policyengine.github.io/policyengine-taxsim/); [PolicyBench mirror](https://tgfed.nhyvvfeijnh.workers.dev/PolicyEngine/policybench)). A second-hand pull request note cites a residual Colorado gap of about $65 against TaxAct and TAXSIM; that figure comes from a mirror page and is thin. No error rate against filed Colorado returns was found. One design rule deserves an insider's attention: when statute conflicts with a form or agency practice, PolicyEngine follows the statute and logs the conflict ([policyengine-us README](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/PolicyEngine/policyengine-us/main/README.md)). The model can thus be right in law yet differ from what the Department of Revenue assesses. That is acceptable for policy analysis and screening and unacceptable, unreconciled, for return preparation.

**The documented failures are about divergence and deployment, not engines.** Independent re-encoding caught a bug in France's official family benefits implementation, and publishing the French tax calculator in 2016 surfaced a rounding error within days ([arXiv 2103.03198](https://arxiv.org/abs/2103.03198); [Next INpact](https://next.ink/14493/99307-decouverte-dun-bug-dans-code-source-impots/)). Robodebt automated an unlawful income-averaging rule without human review; the scheme was meant to save A$4.7 billion, delivered A$406 million and ended in a A$1.8 billion settlement, by press accounts of the Royal Commission ([Canberra Times](https://www.canberratimes.com.au/story/8261218/robodebt-figures-to-face-civil-and-criminal-prosecution/)). The lesson is that open, testable code is a safeguard and that adverse determinations need human review. Colorado's SB26-189, effective 1 January 2027, reportedly requires meaningful human review of significant automated decisions; whether it covers state agencies was not checked in the bill text ([Eckert Seamans](https://www.eckertseamans.com/legal-updates/colorado-repeals-and-replaces-its-landmark-ai-statute-what-businesses-need-to-know)).

**LLMs draft well and calculate badly.** Georgetown's 2025 experiments found plain prompting scored 0% on income and asset limits when generating machine-readable rules, while retrieval-augmented prompting reached 100% in most categories; the authors conclude models still need "human oversight within an iterative process" ([Digital Government Hub](https://digitalgovernmenthub.org/publications/ai-powered-rules-as-code-experiments-with-public-benefits-policy/)). TaxCalcBench found frontier models compute fewer than a third of simplified federal returns correctly ([arXiv 2507.16126](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.16126)). These tests used 2024 and 2025 models, and no independent 2026 evaluation for state tax law was found. The implication cuts both ways: AI assistance lowers encoding cost, which weakens the "too expensive to build" objection, and it raises the value of an independent test oracle, because the same model can write both the rule and its test.

**Code has no legal authority anywhere, and Colorado already owns the fix.** Every precedent surveyed kept code subordinate to text; New Zealand's independent study concluded "legislation should not be code, but law as code is the future" ([Scoop](https://www.scoop.co.nz/stories/AK2103/S00275/legislation-should-not-be-code-but-law-as-code-is-the-future-legal-researchers.htm)). In Colorado only a private letter ruling binds the department, and only for its recipient ([CDOR letter rulings guidance](https://tax.colorado.gov/requesting-a-general-information-letter-or-private-letter-ruling)). A published code base would sit at or below a general information letter. Sarah Lawsky's point answers the agency worry about being pinned down: forms and worksheets already are algorithms in which the government makes the judgment calls, so open code exposes commitments that already exist ([TaxProf Blog](https://taxprofblog.aals.org/2024/08/14/grimmelmann-reviews-lawskys-coding-the-code-catala-and-computationally-accessible-tax-law/)). Blank and Osofsky recommend penalty relief for people who rely on automated guidance ([Cornell Law Review](https://publications.lawschool.cornell.edu/lawreview/?p=2653)). The GIS hold-harmless in C.R.S. 39-26-105.3 is a ready drafting model for an income tax analogue ([C.R.S. 39-26-105.3, unofficial mirror](https://colorado.public.law/statutes/crs_39-26-105.3)). Open-textured tests such as residency, domicile and nexus should enter as attested facts with the statutory test documented beside them, which is how every project examined handles them.

## Governance: split the roles, because every single owner has failed somewhere

**Agency-only ownership is politically fragile.** Direct File earned 94% high satisfaction ratings, cost $41 million in 2025, and was closed anyway; Colorado had been due to join in 2026 ([Route Fifty](https://www.route-fifty.com/digital-government/2026/09/state-efforts-fill-void-left-federal-direct-file-are-underway/416234/)). Sources differ on 2025 usage, from about 296,500 returns to over 309,000, and the Governor's letter cited more than 750,000 users; the discrepancy is unresolved. Open publication was the insurance policy: the code outlived the program, and a Virginia law signed in March 2026 directs its tax department to consider compatibility with it. Colorado's own delivery capacity is unsettled, since the Office of Information Technology laid off 173 employees in a May 2026 overhaul ([KUNC](https://www.kunc.org/politics/2026-05-29/colorado-overhauls-state-it-office-lays-off-173-employees-after-negative-feedback)). Agencies also resist on principle: France's tax authority argued that source code would expose its readings of doctrine, which is as much an argument for publication as against it ([Next INpact](https://next.ink/news/95159-le-fisc-rechigne-a-)).

**Nonprofit-only ownership is financially fragile.** Benefits Data Trust closed in August 2024 and laid off all 273 staff two years after a $20 million unrestricted gift, leaving dependent states exposed ([Philadelphia Inquirer](https://www.inquirer.com/health/benefits-data-trust-bdt-failed-acquirer-closing-august-24-20240730.html)). PolicyEngine's funders and budget were not found, which is itself a diligence gap for anyone proposing to depend on it. Advocacy groups carry an added cost in perceived alignment: the organisations that backed Amendment 87 would not be credible neutral stewards of its encoding.

**What lasted was absorbed onto a permanent budget.** France's LexImpact began as an incubator project on OpenFisca and became a unit of the National Assembly in 2020, letting deputies cost a rate-schedule change in about a minute ([Next INpact](https://next.ink/brief-article/lassemblee-nationale-pousse-leximpact-logiciel-libre-de-simulation-fiscale/)). Mon-entreprise was internalised by the social contribution collector ([Genèse de Publicodes](https://hackmd.io/@laem/genese-publicodes)). The pilots in New Zealand, Canada and New South Wales lived in innovation units and produced learning; no independent evaluation or evidence of scaled adoption was found for any of them. New Zealand's lab drew its own lesson that code written after drafting becomes "a siloed version of the rules" ([Service Innovation Lab](https://serviceinnovationlab.github.io/projects/legislation-as-code)). The OECD still calls rules as code "an early stage concept" with unresolved legal status ([OECD OPSI](https://oecd-opsi.org/publications/cracking-the-code/)). No US state revenue department was found publishing income tax logic, and no US legislature piloting code alongside bills; both are absences after limited search.

**The defensible Colorado design is layered.** An open code base maintained by a nonprofit or university consortium supplies independence and speed. The Department of Revenue supplies test cases and review, since it already runs an annual e-file and substitute-forms acceptance process for vendors ([DOR Efilers Handbook](https://tax.colorado.gov/sites/tax/files/documents/Efilers_Handbook_2025.docx)). Legislative Council Staff, which already estimates credit costs bill by bill, consumes the model for fiscal notes on the LexImpact pattern. A line-item appropriation or statutory duty provides the floor philanthropy cannot. The costs are governance overhead and diffuse accountability for errors, and a risk that staff-written code is misread as legislative intent. Opposition will depend on framing. Documented industry resistance targets government filing products: Intuit spent about $3 million and H&R Block $2.4 million on federal lobbying in 2025, across all issues ([Bloomberg Government](https://news.bgov.com/daily-tax-report/tax-prep-gop-aligned-groups-lobby-to-end-irs-direct-file)). An "open specification and test suite" should draw less fire than a state filing tool; that is inference, and no Colorado lobbying record was found.

## Start with income tax credits and brackets, and contribute upstream

**Income tax is engine-ready; sales tax is not an engine problem.** The income tax and its credits are annual, household-level formulas, which is exactly what PolicyEngine computes, and most of the work is already done. The live Colorado use cases sit there too: SimpleFile, built by Code for America with the department in July 2026, exists to deliver the child tax credit and Family Affordability credit to non-filers ([Code for America](https://codeforamerica.org/?p=17009)). Sales tax is per transaction, per address and per product across layered jurisdictions, with taxability set by dozens of councils. No open engine addresses it, and audit protection attaches only to the state's own lookup. The right sales tax ask is a data one: published rate, boundary and taxability files with the hold-harmless extended to file-based use. The planned 1 January 2027 return of bulk lookup is the natural window to make it.

**Contribute upstream, with two hedges.** Upstream contribution buys the federal base, the validation harness and immediate benefit to existing Colorado users, and it avoids a fork that drifts as the law changes every session. The prices are AGPL copyleft, a fixed statute-over-forms rule, review on someone else's schedule, and dependence on one nonprofit whose leadership is also building a successor format; the README confirms the code base is mid-migration. The first hedge is architectural: keep a thin Colorado layer for anything upstream does not model, and call the engine over its API, as MyFriendBen does. The second is the more important: maintain a Colorado test-case corpus, built from form worked examples and, if the department will release them, its e-file acceptance scenarios, independent of any engine. That corpus validates upstream code today, survives a change of engine, and is the natural place to reconcile statute-first results with what the department's forms assess. Catala-style literate encoding earns its cost only for a small number of contested provisions where a legal audience needs section-by-section correspondence. The missing PTC rebate is an obvious first contribution.

**Amendment 87 changes the order of work.** The measure sets rates of 3.7% up to $25,000, 4.2% to $100,000, 4.4% to $500,000, 7.4% to $750,000, 7.9% to $1 million and 8.4% above, for tax years beginning on or after 1 January 2027, and applies the same schedule to business profits ([Colorado Sun](https://coloradosun.com/2026/11/06/amendment-87-results-passes-graduated-income-tax/)). The Associated Press called it on 6 November with a lead of about 51.4% to 48.6%; the competing 4.4% cap, Proposition 136, was reported defeated, so the conflicting-measures rule does not apply. Three consequences follow. First, the rate becomes a bracket table, and the highest-value artefact this winter is a dated parameter file that records the schedule with status "reported passed, uncertified" and holds open the fields no source answers: thresholds by filing status, indexing, and the interaction with the TABOR temporary rate reduction. Second, effort should go to tax year 2027, not to perfecting flat-rate tax year 2026; returns for 2026 still use the flat rate, and the first graduated returns are filed in early 2028, which gives a full year to build and test before anyone files, though withholding tables need attention before January 2027 on my inference. Third, the TABOR refund machinery is shrinking as a modelling priority: Proposition NN and Proposition 137, both reported passed, retain or redirect revenue that would otherwise be refunded ([Aspen Times](https://aspentimes.com/news/colorado-ballot-measures-midterm-election/)). The 2027 session is also the governance window. Lawmakers will be budgeting the new revenue, reported as about $2 billion a year by one outlet and $2.7 billion by another, and a short statute could set an owner, a licence, an appropriation and an income tax reliance safe harbour while one shared, tested specification of the new brackets is visibly useful to the department, legislative staff and vendors alike. The counter-risk is building against a moving target: the statutory brackets can be amended by the legislature, and certification is still pending.

## What is unverified or thinly sourced

**Election facts.** Every vote figure is unofficial and comes from news reports of 3 to 6 November 2026; no source states that any result is certified, and reported shares for Amendment 87 range from 51.37% to 51.64%. The Secretary of State's results site and the Blue Book could not be read. The simple-majority threshold rests on three news sources against one contrary Ballotpedia statement. The constitutional and statutory sections amended were not confirmed from a primary source. No report of a recount request or post-election lawsuit was found, which is not proof of absence. No Department of Revenue guidance on implementation was found.

**Colorado institutional facts.** Whether the Sales and Use Tax Simplification Task Force was extended past 1 July 2026 is unverified; no extension bill was found, and lapse is an inference from absence. The GIS vendor, the department's core tax system vendor, contract terms and ownership of the boundary layer are unconfirmed. Whether the General Assembly has designated any electronic statute record official under the electronic legal material act is unconfirmed. The LexisNexis contract and site terms were not read. No Colorado case law on estoppel against the department and no general penalty defence for reliance on its written advice were located. SB26-189 is known only from law-firm summaries. No Colorado cost estimate exists in the sources for any layer.

**Engine and precedent facts.** PolicyEngine's test coverage, the tax years its Colorado credits cover, its treatment of part-year and non-resident filers, its contribution rules and its finances were not retrieved; the PTC gap comes from a path-name search that would miss an oddly named file or an open pull request. The $65 Colorado discrepancy is second-hand. Whether France's tax authority runs the academic compiler in production today is unconfirmed. Robodebt and Michigan figures come from press coverage and conflict across sources. The relationship between the "Axiom Foundation" and the "Rules Foundation" is unresolved. Several structured-text claims rest on search extracts, including DC's designation of GitHub as its authoritative source and the Secretary of State's bulk-extraction prohibition, which dates from a 2012 rule on a different subject.

## Conclusion

The four layers do not need to move together, and treating them as one program is the main way to fail. Parameters and test cases are cheap, engine-neutral and immediately useful; rules are mostly an upstream contribution problem; structured text and sales tax data are publisher problems that outsiders can pilot and advocate for but cannot authenticate. The pattern across France, Direct File and Benefits Data Trust is that openness protects the asset while institutional absorption protects the service, so a Colorado effort should secure both: an open licence from day one and a named public owner with a budget line.

Amendment 87 supplies something no precedent had at the start: a discrete, dated, statewide change that the department, legislative staff, vendors and advocates must all implement at once, with a year of lead time before the first return. A shared bracket table and test corpus for tax year 2027 would prove the model on the smallest possible surface. If that works, the harder asks follow from demonstrated value: a reliance safe harbour modelled on the GIS statute, published acceptance tests, and structured output from the Revisor and the Secretary of State. The open questions that would most change this advice are the measure's certified text on filing status and indexing, and whether the department is willing to publish its test scenarios.
