## 1. What is the current publication and licensing status of the Colorado Revised Statutes?

### Takeaway
The statutory text is treated as public domain, the state stopped copyrighting editorial/ancillary material and stopped charging for its statutory database on 4 March 2016, and the database is available free on request, but only as SGML text files in a zip, via a request form under C.R.S. 2-5-118, not as an open bulk download, API, or XML/versioned feed. LexisNexis remains the contract printer and host of the free public-access site; the only "official" statutes are the General Assembly's own publication, and any republisher is statutorily barred from calling its copy official.

### Cited Findings
- The Office of Legislative Legal Services (OLLS), under the Committee on Legal Services, "edits, collates, and revises the laws enacted each session, with annotations, and prints them each year"; the official set is published annually in a softbound edition. — [Colorado General Assembly, C.R.S. page](https://content.leg.colorado.gov/agencies/office-legislative-legal-services/colorado-revised-statutes)
- The General Assembly's page describes LexisNexis as "the current printer" and host: "The Colorado Revised Statutes are available on the internet as hosted by LexisNexis (the current printer)." Official printed sets are bought from LexisNexis. — [Colorado General Assembly, C.R.S. page](https://content.leg.colorado.gov/agencies/office-legislative-legal-services/colorado-revised-statutes)
- Title-by-title downloads are offered by the legislature itself: "You may download DOCX, PDF, or HTM copies of the Colorado Revised Statutes for personal use." The download link slug is "2026-crs-titles-download", indicating a 2026 edition is posted (the page text itself does not state the edition year). — [Colorado General Assembly, C.R.S. page](https://content.leg.colorado.gov/agencies/office-legislative-legal-services/colorado-revised-statutes)
- For anyone wishing to "publish, reprint, or distribute" the statutes, the page points to a separate "Colorado Revised Statutes Data" page and cites C.R.S. 2-5-118. — [Colorado General Assembly, C.R.S. page](https://content.leg.colorado.gov/agencies/office-legislative-legal-services/colorado-revised-statutes)
- The statutory database is "available only as text files in SGML format" and is provided "in a zip file to the requestor without modification"; requests go through a Google Form. The page does not say which years are available or whether annotations are inside the data set. — [Colorado Revised Statutes Data page](http://content.leg.colorado.gov/agencies/office-legislative-legal-services/colorado-revised-statutes-data)
- The same page states that as of 4 March 2016 the Committee on Legal Services stopped copyrighting the original publications and editorial work ancillary to the C.R.S., and suspended fees/costs for use of the statutory database. — [Colorado Revised Statutes Data page](http://content.leg.colorado.gov/agencies/office-legislative-legal-services/colorado-revised-statutes-data)
- Republication conditions on that page: it "must be done accurately"; a republication may state it is an officially sanctioned publication using the official text, but "may not contain any notice or other indication that it is the official statutes of the state of Colorado." The page also warns that "Links to said data may not be functional at this time" because legacy session data is being migrated. Contact listed: Yelana Love, Revisor of Statutes. — [Colorado Revised Statutes Data page](http://content.leg.colorado.gov/agencies/office-legislative-legal-services/colorado-revised-statutes-data)
- Background to the 2016 decision (article dated 4 March 2016): the Committee on Legal Services voted unanimously to stop charging for the annually updated database of the C.R.S. and ancillary information (source notes, editor's notes) and to stop copyrighting that ancillary information. OLLS had charged vendors $2,000 to $6,000 depending on version; in the prior year LexisNexis and two other vendors bought it, yielding $16,000 for the General Fund; historically up to eight vendors bought it. A Colorado Open Records Act request the previous year had already obtained the database at no charge. — [Colorado Freedom of Information Coalition, 2016](https://coloradofoic.org/colorado-statutes-database-should-be-free-lawmakers-decide/)
- Then-Revisor Jennifer Gilroy's stated positions in 2016: there is no copyright on the laws themselves; copyright on ancillary information was unnecessary and hard to enforce because the state already publishes it online; "taxpayer dollars pay for the creation of these original works"; and "Accuracy is my biggest concern." The article notes someone would need the database itself to build a new application. — [Colorado Freedom of Information Coalition, 2016](https://coloradofoic.org/colorado-statutes-database-should-be-free-lawmakers-decide/)
- C.R.S. 2-5-118 (as summarized from the 2024 text on a third-party mirror): (1)(a) the statutes prepared and enacted under article 5 are the official statutes and the only publication admissible as evidence of the statutes in Colorado courts, of which courts take judicial notice; (1)(b) the committee authorizes softbound printing, may work with the printer on electronic formats, may provide the statutory database to others, and recognizes others may publish without the official database; (2) users of the official database must tell the committee what portions they will publish and whether they want ancillary publications, and must reproduce them accurately; (2)(c) the committee may charge a fee covering state property interests and "use of any material copyrighted by the state"; (2.5) publishing ancillary publications requires a prior written application; (4) stated purposes include ensuring copies accurately reflect the law and preventing unofficial publications from being mistaken for official; (5) only database-based publications may carry an "officially sanctioned" notice and none may claim to be the official statutes; (6) anyone may publish up to 200 sections for educational purposes. SB 11-261 (eff. 23 May 2011) deleted several subsections. — [C.R.S. 2-5-118 via public.law (unofficial mirror)](https://colorado.public.law/statutes/crs_2-5-118)
- A search extract of Colorado legislative commentary states that the General Assembly views the C.R.S., including arrangement, headnotes and numbering, as public domain, while state law authorizes the Committee on Legal Services to copyright "not the statutes" but the original publications and editorial work. (Search extract; the LegiSource article itself returned HTTP 404 when fetched.) — [LegiSource, "Who Owns the Law? The Colorado Perspective on Copyright and State Statutes"](https://legisource.net/?p=2561)
- The Committee's publications contract with LexisNexis requires LexisNexis to host a fully annotated, searchable version of the C.R.S. on the internet (search extract). — [LegiSource](https://legisource.net/?p=2561)
- Conflicting characterization: the AALL state online legal information survey says LexisNexis is the official publisher of the C.R.S. and "reserves all rights over CRS" (search extract; date of the AALL entry not verified). This conflicts with the General Assembly's own description of LexisNexis as "printer", with UELMA's designation of the General Assembly as official publisher, and with the 2016 copyright suspension. — [AALL Colorado survey](https://community.aallnet.org/digitalaccesstolegalinformationcommittee/stateonlinelegalinformation/colorado); contradicted by [Colorado Revised Statutes Data page](http://content.leg.colorado.gov/agencies/office-legislative-legal-services/colorado-revised-statutes-data)
- December 2021: at the request of Carl Malamud of Public.Resource.Org, the Samuelson-Glushko Technology Law & Policy Clinic (TLPC) at Colorado Law published a policy brief on access to Colorado law under the government edicts doctrine. Its recommendations: amend C.R.S. provisions and the (then pending) LexisNexis publishing contract, which the brief says "incorrectly assert copyright over the law" and block digital downloading of statutes from the LexisNexis platform; finish Colorado's in-house project to publish the statutes with XML access; and provide uniform, centralized, searchable databases permitting bulk downloads in the best available format. — [TLPC, 9 Dec 2021](https://tlpc.colorado.edu/tlpc-releases-policy-brief-on-access-to-colorado-law-and-the-government-edicts-doctrine/); full brief (not machine-readable in this session): [TLPC brief PDF](https://tlpc.colorado.edu/wp-content/uploads/2021/12/2021.12.09-Colorado-Edicts-Policy-Brief-final-with-Appendix.pdf)
- Public.Resource.Org corresponded with Colorado as early as 30 May 2013: a letter to the Senate President and the OLLS Director refers to a copy of the C.R.S. provided as a scanned version alongside XML-encoded files (search extract; letter not read in full). — [Public.Resource.Org letter, 2013](https://law.resource.org/pub/us/code/ga/pro_v_georgia/discovery/PRO_Georgia/ROG_5/colorado.letter.pdf)
- Georgia v. Public.Resource.Org, Inc. (decided 27 April 2020, 5-4): annotations in Georgia's official code are not copyrightable because they are authored by an arm of the legislature in the course of its legislative duties; under the government edicts doctrine, officials empowered to speak with the force of law cannot be "authors" of works they create in their official duties, even non-binding explanatory material. — [Supreme Court opinion](https://www.supremecourt.gov/opinions/19pdf/590us1r30_3f14.pdf); [Wiley client alert](https://wiley.law/alert-Georgia-v-Public-Resource-Org-Inc-Official-Annotations-of-State-Codes-Not-Protected-by-Copyright)
- Commentary reads the holding as limited: the doctrine applies only to works created by those with authority to make or interpret the law (judges and legislators/legislative arms), not to every government or private work. — [Wiley client alert](https://wiley.law/alert-Georgia-v-Public-Resource-Org-Inc-Official-Annotations-of-State-Codes-Not-Protected-by-Copyright)

### Inferences
- Because OLLS (a legislative staff agency under the Committee on Legal Services) "edits, collates, and revises ... with annotations", Colorado's annotations appear to be legislative-arm authored, which would put them in the same position as Georgia's under the 2020 decision; Colorado had in any case already stopped claiming copyright in ancillary material in 2016. So copyright is a low legal risk for republishing C.R.S. Title 39 text and state-authored notes. Any value-added content authored by LexisNexis itself on its hosted site (and that site's terms of use) is a separate matter not resolved by the cases above.
- The practical barriers are format and process, not ownership: SGML-only, annual snapshot, request form, "without modification" zip, and no stated version history. A Title 39 structured-data project would have to (a) request and convert SGML, or (b) parse the DOCX/HTM title downloads, each year.
- The DOCX/PDF/HTM downloads are labelled "for personal use", while republication is routed through the 2-5-118 process. A project that republishes Title 39 should expect to follow the 2-5-118 statement-of-portions step and must avoid any "official" labelling; the statute's accuracy duty applies to every republisher.
- 2-5-118(2)(c) and (2.5) still contain fee and copyrighted-material language on the books; the 2016 action was a Committee "suspension", so the openness rests on committee policy rather than statutory repeal and could in principle be reversed without legislation.
- The "Links ... may not be functional" migration warning suggests the legislature's web/data infrastructure was in flux in 2026.

### Gaps
- Could not read the TLPC brief or confirm the current LexisNexis contract term, renewal date, or terms-of-use language; could not confirm whether the 2021 recommendations were adopted or whether the "in-house project to publish the statutes with XML access" was completed. The data page still says SGML only, which suggests XML publication had not shipped as of the page's current text.
- Did not verify the text of C.R.S. 2-5-115 (copyright provision) or whether any bill since 2016 amended 2-5-115/2-5-118.
- Did not inspect the LexisNexis public-access site's current terms of use, or whether it permits scraping/bulk download.
- Whether the SGML data set includes annotations, source notes and editor's notes, and whether prior-year data sets are obtainable (needed for point-in-time history), is not stated on the page.
- No source found on whether Colorado publishes historical (superseded) statute versions officially online other than prior-year title downloads.

## 2. How is the Code of Colorado Regulations published, and is it available in bulk or structured form?

### Takeaway
The Secretary of State publishes the official CCR online only, as digitally certified PDFs per rule, with version history back to 2007; no bulk download, API, XML or other structured format was found, and older SOS rule language prohibits automated bulk extraction from its site while offering bulk data on request for a fee. For 1 CCR 201 (Department of Revenue tax rules) this means structured text must be extracted from PDFs.

### Cited Findings
- The Secretary of State "publishes the official version of Colorado's administrative rules" in the CCR, and publishes the official Code and Register online as required by law. — [SOS CCR FAQs](https://coloradosos.gov/pubs/CCR/FAQs.html)
- Rules are published as PDF documents. The Colorado Register is published on the 10th and 25th of each month and contains rulemaking notices, proposed/new/amended rules and Attorney General opinions. — [SOS CCR FAQs](https://coloradosos.gov/pubs/CCR/FAQs.html)
- Version history: the online CCR contains past versions back to 2007 in an "All versions" table sorted by effective date; changes since 1 April 2007 appear in "Editor's Notes"; for pre-2007 rules users are sent to the adopting agency, the Supreme Court Library or State Archives. — [SOS CCR FAQs](https://coloradosos.gov/pubs/CCR/FAQs.html)
- Authentication: as of 31 March 2014 each published regulation carries a digital certificate showing certification date/time and indicating the document is unaltered from the official record, verifiable in Adobe Reader. — [SOS CCR FAQs](https://coloradosos.gov/pubs/CCR/FAQs.html)
- eDocket is described as a "real-time log of agency rulemaking filings", searchable by tracking number or by agency and date range. — [SOS CCR FAQs](https://coloradosos.gov/pubs/CCR/FAQs.html)
- The online CCR has been the official version since 2007 and print publication ceased in September 2019 (search extracts from Colorado Lawyer research columns and the AALL survey; the two extracts were not fully reconciled as to exact dates). — [Colorado Lawyer, "Researching Colorado Regulations"](https://cl.cobar.org/departments/researching-colorado-regulations/); [AALL Colorado survey](https://community.aallnet.org/digitalaccesstolegalinformationcommittee/stateonlinelegalinformation/colorado)
- Agencies draft rules in Microsoft Word using an SOS styling manual and template. — [SOS Rules Styling Manual](https://www.coloradosos.gov/pubs/CCR/files/rules_styling_manual.pdf)
- A 2012 SOS rule document (lobbyist rules, not CCR-specific) states that automated extraction of bulk data from the Secretary of State's website by "robots" or "data mining" is prohibited and that the SOS will provide bulk data on request for a fee (search extract; may be outdated and may not govern CCR pages). — [SOS, 2012 adopted rules PDF](https://coloradosos.gov/pubs/rule_making/files/2012/20120926_LobbyistRulesAsAdopted.pdf)
- Third parties (e.g., Justia) republish the CCR as HTML. — [Justia CCR](https://regulations.justia.com/states/colorado/1505/1505/)

### Inferences
- The CCR already has two things the C.R.S. lacks online: official per-rule point-in-time versions (since 2007) and cryptographic authentication. What it lacks is structure: each version is a PDF, so 1 CCR 201 rules would require PDF-to-structure conversion, with the usual risks (tables, numbering, footnotes) and no official machine-readable diff.
- Because agencies author in Word against a common template, a source-format pipeline (Word to XML) is conceivable upstream, but nothing found indicates the SOS exposes Word sources publicly.
- A scraper-based ingestion of the CCR site may conflict with SOS site terms; a formal data request to rules@coloradosos.gov is the lower-risk path.

### Gaps
- No source found confirming or denying a CCR bulk download, API, or XML export as of 2026; no CCR-specific terms of use or copyright statement located.
- Did not verify how the Department of Revenue itself posts tax rules (its own site vs. SOS) or whether DOR offers any structured format.
- Whether the SOS digital certificate satisfies downstream verification for derived (non-PDF) copies is not addressed anywhere found.

## 3. USLM vs. Akoma Ntoso / LegalDocML vs. simpler formats (JSON, Markdown in git)

### Takeaway
USLM is a US-specific, GPO-maintained XML schema with active 2026 development and a flexible hierarchy that fits US codes, but no evidence was found of state-level adoption; Akoma Ntoso is the international OASIS standard with broader tooling and multi-country use, but the literature reports low adoption relative to its age, high conversion cost and skill requirements, and model mismatches when retrofitting existing corpora. No sourced comparative evaluation of JSON/Markdown-in-git approaches was retrieved; DC's XML-in-git is the closest documented example and it uses XML, not Markdown.

### Cited Findings
- USLM is an XML schema for US legislative documents, published by GPO on GitHub in support of the Legislative Branch XML Working Group under 2 U.S.C. 181. As of November 2026 the main branch holds approved schema versions 2.0.14 through 2.0.17 and 2.1.0; proposed branches (2.1.1, 2.1.2) add committee/conference reports and CBO cost estimates; the stylesheet was updated 4 Sep 2026. Stated policy: avoid breaking changes, keep all adopted versions available, version as major.minor.point. — [usgpo/uslm on GitHub](https://github.com/usgpo/uslm)
- The Office of the Law Revision Counsel released the United States Code in XML (USLM) in July 2013. — [Library of Congress, In Custodia Legis, 2014](https://blogs.loc.gov/law/2014/07/making-legislative-information-accessible-discoverable-and-usable/); schema documentation: [USLM User Guide (OLRC)](https://usc-cdn.house.gov/download/resources/USLM-User-Guide.pdf)
- USLM is described as a second-generation schema derived from Akoma Ntoso principles but with a separate schema tailored to US legislative structure (search extracts from secondary sources). — [Vaquill guide (secondary)](https://www.vaquill.ai/blog/uslm-vs-akoma-ntoso-legislative-xml-guide); [parlamento.ai AKN national profiles (secondary)](https://diff.parlamento.ai/docs/akn/national-profiles)
- Structural comparison from a 2015 OASIS technical-committee thread: USLM (and ELI) have an open hierarchy whereas Akoma Ntoso has a fixed hierarchy; USLM at that time had no language provision while AKN uses ISO 639-3 codes. (2015; schema versions have since changed.) — [OASIS legalcitem-technical list, 2015](https://lists.oasis-open.org/archives/legalcitem-technical/201501/msg00002.html)
- The Library of Congress ran two data challenges in 2013 (markup of US legislation in Akoma Ntoso; legislative XML data mapping) to test AKN against US bill XML. — [Library of Congress, 2014](https://blogs.loc.gov/law/2014/07/making-legislative-information-accessible-discoverable-and-usable/); follow-up: [Library of Congress, 2015](https://blogs.loc.gov/law/2015/01/legislative-data-challenges-one-year-later/)
- A 2021 academic paper states that Akoma Ntoso's adoption rate "has been strikingly low", attributing this to the cost of converting natural-language legislation to XML (described as an eight-step, laborious process starting with legal analysis usually done on paper) and to the scarcity of legal experts with XML skills. — [arXiv 2108.08678, "The Legislative Recipe"](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2108.08678)
- Akoma Ntoso was developed under UN DESA; an AKN-based amendment tool is used at the European Parliament; national uses listed in secondary sources include the Italian Senate, Uruguay and others (search extracts). — [arXiv 2108.08678](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2108.08678); [parlamento.ai AKN national profiles (secondary)](https://diff.parlamento.ai/docs/akn/national-profiles)
- A developer account (c. 2015, search extract) of converting UK legislation to AKN reported the main difficulty was a mismatch between the UK's native CLML paragraph model and Akoma Ntoso's. — [OASIS legaldocml list](https://lists.oasis-open.org/archives/legaldocml/201309/msg00006.html)
- A 2025-era commentary on Brazil's LexML argues AKN adoption has been quiet and uneven and that the main obstacle is institutional rather than technical: "Digital standards cannot integrate what political organizations themselves keep separate." (Commentary/opinion.) — [Modern Parliament substack](https://modparl.substack.com/p/when-law-becomes-data)
- The DC Council publishes its statutes and code as XML in public git repositories (law-xml: uncodified laws plus the code as of 10 March 2016; a codified sibling repo; an HTML sibling repo); the law-xml repository shows 834 commits, 354 stars, 32 forks and includes its own schemas folder. — [DCCouncil/law-xml](https://github.com/DCCouncil/law-xml)
- Colorado's own statutory database is SGML, a predecessor of XML. — [Colorado Revised Statutes Data page](http://content.leg.colorado.gov/agencies/office-legislative-legal-services/colorado-revised-statutes-data)

### Inferences
Pros/cons assembled from the findings above (analysis, not sourced claims):

| Option | Advantages | Disadvantages |
|---|---|---|
| USLM | Designed for US code hierarchy (title/article/part/section/subsection); open hierarchy tolerates Colorado's "title-article-part-section" numbering (e.g., 39-22-104); actively maintained by GPO in 2026 with a no-breaking-change policy; public schema, CSS and user guide; same model as the US Code, easing cross-references to the IRC from Title 39 | Governed by federal legislative-branch bodies with no state seat; no state adopter identified in this research, so no state-profile, tooling or peer community; versioning/point-in-time semantics largely left to the publisher; smaller third-party tool ecosystem than AKN |
| Akoma Ntoso / LegalDocML | International OASIS standard; richer built-in model for lifecycle, versions, references and metadata; used by multiple parliaments and by EU institutions; vendor tools exist | Fixed hierarchy and paragraph model can mismatch existing corpora (UK experience); literature reports low adoption, high conversion labor and need for XML-literate legal staff; large, complex schema usually requires a local profile, which erodes interoperability |
| Custom XML in git (DC model) | Proven in a US jurisdiction; diffs and history come free from git; schema can match local drafting conventions exactly | Bespoke schema means bespoke tooling and no interchange; dependence on the implementing organization |
| JSON / Markdown in git | Lowest barrier for developers and for rules-as-code projects; trivial diffs; cheap to host | No standard semantics for amendments, effective dates, or citations; Markdown loses hierarchy fidelity (nested subsections, tables, notes); not a recognized authentication or archival format; every consumer invents its own schema |

- Since Colorado's upstream is SGML (statutes) and PDF/Word (rules), any target format requires a custom converter; the marginal cost difference between emitting USLM, AKN or JSON from one parsed tree is small compared with the cost of parsing and of annual re-validation. A common pattern is to hold one canonical XML form and derive JSON/Markdown views.
- The tax-specific argument for USLM is cross-reference alignment: Title 39 income tax provisions reference the Internal Revenue Code, which is natively USLM.

### Gaps
- No source retrieved identifying any US state legislature that publishes its code in USLM or Akoma Ntoso; NCSL or vendor (e.g., Xcential, Propylon) material was not searched.
- No dollar figures retrieved for USLM or AKN implementation projects.
- No sourced evaluation of Markdown/JSON-in-git statute projects (successes or failures) retrieved; the JSON/Markdown row above is analysis only.
- Whether eCFR/CFR bulk data is offered in USLM was not confirmed (the GPO repo page read did not list the CFR).
- Background, unverified: California's legislature has used an Akoma Ntoso-derived drafting system; legislation.gov.uk offers AKN as an export alongside native CLML; the US House Clerk publishes bills in USLM alongside legacy bill DTD XML. None of these were confirmed in this session.

## 4. Precedents for point-in-time versioned open legal codes: what worked, what failed, what it cost

### Takeaway
The durable precedents are publisher-side (DC Council with Open Law Library; UK National Archives' legislation.gov.uk; the federal US Code and eCFR), where the government's own workflow emits the structured data; volunteer or grant-funded re-publication projects (The State Decoded and its Maryland/Virginia instances) depended on short-term philanthropy and on scraping whatever the state published. Even well-resourced official systems carry a visible currency backlog. Hard cost figures were largely not found.

### Cited Findings
- DC: the DC Council's law is held as XML in public GitHub repositories (uncodified laws and code baseline of 10 March 2016; codified code; HTML rendering); 834 commits on the law-xml repo as of Nov 2026. — [DCCouncil/law-xml](https://github.com/DCCouncil/law-xml)
- DC: a 2018 Ars Technica piece (search extract via archive) reports that DC made GitHub the authoritative digital source for DC laws, that a member of the public had a typo correction accepted by pull request, and that the Open Law Library process shortened publication to about a week after enactment. — [Ars Technica, 2018 (archived)](https://webcf.waybackmachine.org/web/20211001074126/https://arstechnica.com/tech-policy/2018/11/how-i-changed-the-law-with-a-github-pull-request)
- Open Law Library is a 501(c)(3) public charity that helps governments publish official laws directly; its GitHub organization (34 repositories) shows active development in Oct-Nov 2026, including "taf" (The Archive Framework, Python, AGPL-3.0) and "taf-server" ("Preservation, Authentication, Access", Rust), i.e., tooling aimed at the UELMA triad of authentication, preservation and access. — [Open Law Library on GitHub](https://github.com/openlawlibrary)
- UK: legislation.gov.uk holds many primary legislation types in "revised" form (amendments incorporated), with a base date of 1 February 1991 inherited from Statutes in Force. New legislation's effects are tabulated in "Changes to Legislation", which "can take up to six weeks"; the aim is to incorporate amendments within three months of coming into force; unapplied effects are flagged by banner. Most secondary legislation is not revised and is held only as originally made. Content is under the Open Government Licence v3.0. — [legislation.gov.uk, Understanding legislation](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/understanding-legislation)
- UK: site notices state revised legislation "may not be fully up to date", and that changes not yet applied by the editorial team are shown only on the latest/prospective version and "are therefore not accessible when viewing legislation as at a specific point in time" (search extracts of standard site notices). — [legislation.gov.uk example provision](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2008/28/part/7/2012-03-26)
- US Code: OLRC has published the US Code in USLM XML since July 2013. — [Library of Congress, 2014](https://blogs.loc.gov/law/2014/07/making-legislative-information-accessible-discoverable-and-usable/)
- The State Decoded: created by Waldo Jaquith; funded by a $165,000 Knight News Challenge grant (2011) to expand nationally; first site Virginia Decoded launched in public beta in 2012; a 2013 release added a public API and a standard XML format for legal information; described as free open-source software to display laws online. — [Nieman Lab, 2011](https://niemanlab.org/2011/06/the-state-decoded-news-challenge-winner-makes-the-law-more-user-friendly-and-easier-on-the-eyes); [MediaShift, 2013](https://mediashift.org/2013/02/the-state-decoded-adds-public-api-standard-xml-format-for-legal-info042); [Virginia Decoded launch post](https://statedecoded.com/2012/03/virginia-decoded/)
- The OpenGov Foundation used State Decoded software for Maryland (launched May 2013) and city codes such as Baltimore, employing a contributor to work on it; Jaquith clarified the platform was his project, used by OpenGov. — [Next City](https://nextcity.org/sharedcity/entry/the-art-of-making-city-code-beautiful); [opengovfoundation/baltimorecode](https://www.github.com/opengovfoundation/baltimorecode)
- Sector context: open-government nonprofits have been contracting; the Sunlight Foundation cut staff and suspended tools, and a 2020s Bulwark piece is titled "Open-Government Nonprofits Are Dying Off Just When They're Needed Most" (search extracts; not specific to State Decoded). — [Benton summary of Sunlight cuts](https://www.benton.org/headlines/open-government-advocacy-group-sunlight-foundation-cuts-staff-suspends-reporting-tools); [The Bulwark](https://thebulwark.com/p/open-government-nonprofits-are-dying-off-just-when-needed-most)
- Colorado's fee history gives one data point on revenue forgone by opening data: $16,000 per year from three vendors in 2015. — [Colorado Freedom of Information Coalition, 2016](https://coloradofoic.org/colorado-statutes-database-should-be-free-lawmakers-decide/)
- Third-party mirrors already republish the C.R.S. as HTML with per-year editions (Justia 2016 and 2022 editions seen; public.law states source "Colorado Revised Statutes 2024" and "current through Fall 2025"), demonstrating annual-snapshot granularity and lag in unofficial copies. — [Justia C.R.S. 2022](https://law.justia.com/codes/colorado/2022/title-24/article-71-5/); [public.law](https://colorado.public.law/statutes/crs_2-5-118)

### Inferences
- What worked: models where structured data is the publisher's working format (DC, UK, OLRC). The public repository is then a by-product, drift is impossible by construction, and speed improves (DC: about a week).
- What is fragile: downstream conversion by third parties. It depends on grants (State Decoded: one $165k grant plus one foundation's staff time), on the upstream format staying stable, and on someone re-running and re-validating conversion each cycle.
- Even the best-funded official point-in-time service (UK) has a standing lag (six weeks to tabulate, three-month target to apply) and leaves most secondary legislation unrevised; this is the realistic ceiling for regulations, and is relevant to 1 CCR 201.
- For Colorado, point-in-time granularity is constrained by the sources: annual C.R.S. editions (plus session laws for intra-year changes) and per-rule effective-dated PDFs for the CCR (since 2007). A git history for Title 39 can be annual at best unless session-law amendments are applied by the project itself, which is editorial work that creates drift risk.
- A Title 39-only scope (one title, one CCR chapter) is far smaller than any precedent above; most precedent cost drivers (whole-code conversion, drafting-system integration) scale down, but annual re-validation does not go away.

### Gaps
- No verified cost or staffing figures for Open Law Library/DC, legislation.gov.uk, eCFR, OLRC, or State Decoded maintenance beyond the single Knight grant figure.
- The Open Law Library website (about page) returned 404; the list of partner jurisdictions and its pricing model were not retrieved. DC's "authoritative"/official status statement was not found on the repository page itself; it rests on the Ars Technica extract.
- eCFR could not be fetched (bot block). Background, unverified: eCFR is updated daily, is editorially compiled by OFR/GPO and has historically been labelled not an official legal edition of the CFR, offers point-in-time views from January 2017, and has a public API and bulk XML.
- Current status of The State Decoded, Maryland Decoded and the OpenGov Foundation was not confirmed. Background, unverified: the OpenGov Foundation ceased operations around 2019 and State Decoded sites have largely lapsed.
- Nothing retrieved on Maryland's official code publication, Free Law Project, Cornell LII, or Public.Resource.Org's bulk-code operations (beyond the Colorado items in section 1). Background, unverified: LII and Justia republish state codes from official sources or scrapes with annual lag; Free Law Project focuses on case law (CourtListener) rather than statutes.
- No Colorado-specific instance of State Decoded or Open Law Library engagement was found.

## 5. Does Colorado's legislature publish bills or session laws in structured format, and has any Colorado initiative addressed open legal data?

### Takeaway
No official structured (XML/JSON) feed or API for Colorado bills or session laws was found; the legislature's public outputs are PDF/DOCX/HTM, with an internal SGML statutory database. The relevant Colorado actions are the 2011 statute changes (SB 11-261), the 2016 Committee on Legal Services decision to drop fees and copyright, UELMA (effective for material first published electronically on or after 31 March 2014), and a 2016 open-data bill focused on the Secretary of State; a 2021 clinic brief says an in-house XML statutes project existed but was unfinished.

### Cited Findings
- The legislature's statute outputs: DOCX, PDF and HTM per title for download; SGML database on request. — [Colorado General Assembly, C.R.S. page](https://content.leg.colorado.gov/agencies/office-legislative-legal-services/colorado-revised-statutes); [Colorado Revised Statutes Data page](http://content.leg.colorado.gov/agencies/office-legislative-legal-services/colorado-revised-statutes-data)
- OLLS is directed by statute to prepare the Digest of Bills, Session Laws of Colorado, Red Book and C.R.S. for publication after each session (search extract). — [OLLS duties and responsibilities](https://content.leg.colorado.gov/agencies/office-legislative-legal-services/duties-and-responsibilities)
- The December 2021 TLPC brief recommended that Colorado "finish" an in-house project to publish the statutes with XML access, implying such a project was under way but incomplete in 2021. — [TLPC, 9 Dec 2021](https://tlpc.colorado.edu/tlpc-releases-policy-brief-on-access-to-colorado-law-and-the-government-edicts-doctrine/)
- Structured Colorado bill data is available from a commercial aggregator, not the state: LegiScan publishes Colorado datasets as weekly snapshots in CSV and as JSON API payloads. — [LegiScan Colorado datasets](https://www.legiscan.com/CO/datasets)
- SB 11-261 (effective 23 May 2011) amended C.R.S. 2-5-118, deleting subsection (3)(a) and (5)(b)-(d) and validating earlier contracts; a search extract describes the bill as repealing a requirement to copyright the statutes (enacted status of each provision not independently verified beyond the amendment history note). — [C.R.S. 2-5-118 via public.law](https://colorado.public.law/statutes/crs_2-5-118); [SB 11-261 amended text, LegiScan](https://legiscan.com/CO/text/SB261/id/289423/Colorado-2011-SB261-Amended.pdf)
- 4 March 2016: Committee on Legal Services ended fees and copyright on ancillary material. — [Colorado Freedom of Information Coalition, 2016](https://coloradofoic.org/colorado-statutes-database-should-be-free-lawmakers-decide/)
- HB 16-1014: Colorado legislators moved to formalize a roughly 2.5-year-old pilot open-data program in the Secretary of State's office encouraging agencies to publish machine-readable datasets (search extract; final enactment and current status not verified). — [Colorado Freedom of Information Coalition, HB 16-1014 tag](https://coloradofoic.org/tag/hb-16-1014/)
- Colorado enacted the Uniform Electronic Legal Material Act as C.R.S. Title 24, Article 71.5, applying to legal material in an electronic record designated official and first published electronically on or after 31 March 2014. — [SOS copy of C.R.S. 24-71.5 (unofficial)](https://coloradosos.gov/pubs/info_center/laws/Title24/Title24Article71-5.html)
- The legislature's site warned in 2026 that links to legacy data may be non-functional during migration of legacy session data. — [Colorado Revised Statutes Data page](http://content.leg.colorado.gov/agencies/office-legislative-legal-services/colorado-revised-statutes-data)

### Inferences

## 6. Main risks: drift, authority/authenticity (UELMA), maintenance cost, publisher-owned editorial content

### Takeaway
The dominant risks are non-legal: an unofficial structured copy cannot be the official statutes under C.R.S. 2-5-118, gains no UELMA presumption of accuracy, must be re-derived each year from SGML/PDF sources, and will lag or drift unless the official publishers adopt structured output themselves. Copyright risk for state-authored text and notes is low after the 2016 Committee action and the 2020 Supreme Court decision, with residual uncertainty around LexisNexis-hosted presentation and terms of use.

### Cited Findings
- Authority: only the statutes prepared and enacted under C.R.S. Title 2, Article 5 are the official statutes and the only publication admissible as evidence of the statutes in Colorado courts; other publications may not indicate they are the official statutes; all republishers must reproduce the statutes accurately; a stated statutory purpose is preventing unofficial publications from being mistaken for official. — [C.R.S. 2-5-118 via public.law](https://colorado.public.law/statutes/crs_2-5-118); [Colorado Revised Statutes Data page](http://content.leg.colorado.gov/agencies/office-legislative-legal-services/colorado-revised-statutes-data)
- UELMA official publishers in Colorado: the General Assembly for the constitution, session laws and C.R.S.; the Secretary of State for rules in the CCR. — [SOS copy of C.R.S. 24-71.5 (unofficial)](https://coloradosos.gov/pubs/info_center/laws/Title24/Title24Article71-5.html)
- UELMA duties: an official publisher that publishes legal material only electronically must designate the record official and meet authentication (24-71.5-105), preservation (-107) and public access (-108) requirements; authentication means providing a method to determine the record is unaltered. — [SOS copy of C.R.S. 24-71.5 (unofficial)](https://coloradosos.gov/pubs/info_center/laws/Title24/Title24Article71-5.html); [C.R.S. 24-71.5-105 via public.law](https://colorado.public.law/statutes/crs_24-71.5-105)
- UELMA effect: authenticated legal material is presumed an accurate copy; a party contesting it bears the burden of proving by a preponderance of the evidence that it is not authentic. — [C.R.S. 24-71.5-106 via public.law](https://colorado.public.law/statutes/crs_24-71.5-106)
- UELMA is technology-neutral: the enacting state chooses the authentication technology (official comment, search extract). — [Justia, C.R.S. 24-71.5-105 annotations](https://law.justia.com/codes/colorado/2022/title-24/article-71-5/section-24-71-5-105/)
- In practice the SOS authenticates each CCR rule PDF with a digital certificate (since 31 March 2014); the C.R.S. official version remains the printed softbound set, with certified copies of statute portions available from OLLS at $35/hour staff time (0.25-hour minimum), $0.10/page and $2.00 for a notarized certification letter. — [SOS CCR FAQs](https://coloradosos.gov/pubs/CCR/FAQs.html); [Colorado General Assembly, C.R.S. page](https://content.leg.colorado.gov/agencies/office-legislative-legal-services/colorado-revised-statutes)
- Accuracy was the Revisor's stated primary concern when opening the database in 2016 ("Accuracy is my biggest concern"). — [Colorado Freedom of Information Coalition, 2016](https://coloradofoic.org/colorado-statutes-database-should-be-free-lawmakers-decide/)
- Lag is inherent even in official revised-text services: legislation.gov.uk warns revised text "may not be fully up to date", targets three months to apply amendments, and cannot show unapplied effects in point-in-time views. — [legislation.gov.uk, Understanding legislation](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/understanding-legislation)
- Unofficial Colorado mirrors display stale-edition notices and disclaimers (Justia: "There is a newer version of the Colorado Revised Statutes"; the SOS's own statute copy warns it is not an official copy). — [Justia C.R.S. 2016](https://law.justia.com/codes/colorado/2016/title-24/electronic-transactions/article-71.5/section-24-71.5-105/); [SOS copy of C.R.S. 24-71.5 (unofficial)](https://coloradosos.gov/pubs/info_center/laws/Title24/Title24Article71-5.html)
- Editorial content: Colorado's ancillary material (source notes, editor's notes, cross references, annotations, tables, indices) is prepared by the General Assembly's staff; the Committee stopped copyrighting it in 2016, but C.R.S. 2-5-118(2.5) still requires a prior written application to publish ancillary publications and the committee must find the request "not detrimental" to citizens' interests. — [Colorado Revised Statutes Data page](http://content.leg.colorado.gov/agencies/office-legislative-legal-services/colorado-revised-statutes-data); [C.R.S. 2-5-118 via public.law](https://colorado.public.law/statutes/crs_2-5-118)
- Under Georgia v. Public.Resource.Org, annotations authored by a legislative arm in its legislative duties are not copyrightable; the ruling does not reach works by private parties acting on their own account. — [Supreme Court opinion](https://www.supremecourt.gov/opinions/19pdf/590us1r30_3f14.pdf); [Wiley client alert](https://wiley.law/alert-Georgia-v-Public-Resource-Org-Inc-Official-Annotations-of-State-Codes-Not-Protected-by-Copyright)
- The 2021 TLPC brief asserted that the LexisNexis contract and some C.R.S. provisions still "incorrectly assert copyright over the law" and that the LexisNexis platform withholds digital downloading. — [TLPC, 9 Dec 2021](https://tlpc.colorado.edu/tlpc-releases-policy-brief-on-access-to-colorado-law-and-the-government-edicts-doctrine/)
- Authentication tooling for structured/git-based legal publication exists as open source (Open Law Library's "The Archive Framework" and taf-server: "Preservation, Authentication, Access"), actively maintained in Nov 2026. — [Open Law Library on GitHub](https://github.com/openlawlibrary)
- Sustainability: the State Decoded line of projects relied on a single $165,000 grant and a nonprofit's staff time; open-government nonprofits have since contracted. — [Nieman Lab, 2011](https://niemanlab.org/2011/06/the-state-decoded-news-challenge-winner-makes-the-law-more-user-friendly-and-easier-on-the-eyes); [The Bulwark](https://thebulwark.com/p/open-government-nonprofits-are-dying-off-just-when-needed-most)

### Inferences
- Risk matrix (analysis):
  - Drift from official text: high for a third-party conversion (annual SGML reconversion, PDF parsing for CCR, manual application of session laws); near zero only if OLLS/SOS emit the structured form themselves. Mitigations: automated round-trip comparison against the official DOCX/HTM and CCR PDFs, per-section hashes, visible "as of" dates, and no intra-year editorial consolidation unless clearly flagged.
  - Authority/authenticity: an unofficial structured copy has no evidentiary status (2-5-118) and no UELMA presumption (24-71.5-106). For tax administration this matters if software or rules-as-code is built on the copy: a discrepancy is resolved against the copy. UELMA being technology-neutral means Colorado could authenticate XML/git output (as DC does with Open Law Library tooling), but only the official publisher can do so.
  - A two-publisher problem: statutes (General Assembly) and rules (Secretary of State, authored by the Department of Revenue) have different official publishers, formats, update cycles and authentication methods; a unified Title 39 + 1 CCR 201 dataset necessarily spans both and neither publisher can authenticate the whole.
  - Maintenance cost: unquantified in sources; structurally it is recurring (every session and every rulemaking) rather than one-off, and precedent shows grant-funded maintenance tends to lapse.
  - Editorial content: state-authored notes are legally reusable but procedurally gated (2.5 application); excluding annotations simplifies permissions and reduces volume, at the cost of losing source notes that are useful for point-in-time reconstruction (they record amendment history).
  - Policy reversibility: fee/copyright suspension is committee policy; statutory fee and copyright hooks remain in 2-5-118.
- Upside that these risks trade against (analysis): diffable history, citable section-level identifiers, and direct linkage between statute/rule text and executable tax logic; Title 39's small scope makes a pilot cheap relative to whole-code efforts.

### Gaps
- No source establishes whether the General Assembly has formally designated any electronic C.R.S. record as "official" under 24-71.5-104, or what authentication method (if any) it uses for electronic statutes; the evidence found suggests print remains the official C.R.S. and UELMA obligations are therefore not triggered for the online statutes, but this was not confirmed.
- No documented Colorado incident of harm from drift in an unofficial statute copy was found.
- No cost estimates (staff FTE, vendor contracts) for Colorado to produce XML statutes or structured rules were found; the value and terms of the LexisNexis publications contract were not retrieved.
- LexisNexis public-access site terms of use (scraping, reuse, copyright notice on Colorado pages) not examined.
- Whether a court or the Department of Revenue has ever addressed reliance on unofficial/machine-readable versions of Colorado tax law was not researched.
