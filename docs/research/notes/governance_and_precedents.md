# Governance, legal authority, funding and stewardship of machine-readable Colorado tax law (state of play as of 11 November 2026)

Research note conventions: every dated fact is dated in the bullet. "Secondary" marks claims I could only confirm through news, law-firm alerts or search-result summaries rather than the primary document. Where I could not source a claim, it sits under Gaps, not Findings.

---

## 1. What happened in the main government rules-as-code precedents (France, New Zealand, Canada, Australia, OECD)?

### Takeaway
Every foreign precedent kept coded rules subordinate to the natural-language text, and the durable ones (France's OpenFisca/LexImpact/Mon-entreprise) survived because a permanent institution (parliament, the social-security collector) absorbed the team and budget; the pilots in New Zealand, Canada and New South Wales produced proofs of concept and learning but I found no published independent evaluation or evidence of scaled, mandatory adoption. Robodebt is the cautionary case: the failure was an unlawful rule (income averaging) automated at scale without human review, costing more than it raised and ending in a A$1.8bn settlement.

### Cited Findings

**France**
- Access to the tax authority's code had to be forced: a researcher asked in 2014 for the income-tax simulator source code (partly to help OpenFisca); the CADA (access-to-documents commission) found it communicable, but its opinion is non-binding and the DGFiP initially resisted, arguing among other things that source code can expose the administration's particular readings of tax doctrine and that the code comprised many files needing heavy processing — [Next INpact](https://next.ink/news/95159-le-fisc-rechigne-a-); CADA opinion reproduced at [lexing.law (2015)](https://www.lexing.law/wp-content/uploads/2015/03/30656566.pdf)
- The DGFiP opened the income-tax calculator source (written in its in-house "M" language) on 1 April 2016, in the version that computed tax on 2014 income, at a "CodeImpot" hackathon held 1-2 April 2016 in Paris with three ministers present — [Acteurs publics](https://acteurspublics.fr/articles/le-code-source-de-calcul-des-impots-devoile-au-public-lors-dun-hackathon/); [EU Interoperable Europe / OSOR](https://interoperable-europe.ec.europa.eu/collection/egovernment/document/france-opens-source-code-tax-and-benefits-calculators-increase-transparency)
- Publication immediately surfaced an error: a rounding bug (rounding up where the law required rounding down) was reported during the hackathon, with press reporting the ministry might owe one euro to several million 2014 taxpayers — [Next INpact](https://next.ink/14493/99307-decouverte-dun-bug-dans-code-source-impots/) (secondary; magnitude described as "could")
- The DGFiP now lists source code for income tax (IR), withholding-rate calculation, real-estate wealth tax and other taxes on its open-data page — [impots.gouv.fr](https://www.impots.gouv.fr/ouverture-des-donnees-publiques-de-la-dgfip); mirror repository [calculette-impots-m-source-code](https://data.code.gouv.fr/hosts/GitHub/repositories/GouvernementFR%2Fcalculette-impots-m-source-code)
- Scale and maintainability problem of official code: the French income-tax rules are about 90,000 lines of M that compile to about 535,000 lines of C; academics (Merigoux, Monat, Protzenko) had to write a new compiler ("Mlang") to give M a formal semantics before the published code could be run and analysed outside DGFiP — [A Modern Compiler for the French Tax Code, arXiv 2011.07966](https://arxiv.org/abs/2011.07966v3)
- Independent re-encoding finds bugs in official systems: the Catala project's formalisation of French family benefits "uncovers a bug in the official implementation" — [Catala: A Programming Language for the Law, arXiv 2103.03198](https://arxiv.org/abs/2103.03198v2); [Microsoft Research](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/publication/catala-a-programming-language-for-the-law/)
- OpenFisca is free software under the GNU AGPL, written in Python; the IPP's TAXIPP microsimulation model is built on it — [EU Interoperable Europe: OpenFisca](https://interoperable-europe.ec.europa.eu/collection/justice-law-and-security/solution/openfisca); [IPP TAXIPP guide (Nov 2019)](https://halshs.archives-ouvertes.fr/halshs-02514276v1/file/guide-method-ipp-nov2019-TAXIPP-1-1.pdf)
- LexImpact started as a "start-up d'État" at DINUM built on OpenFisca; in December 2019 the Assemblée nationale's Questure authorised hiring data scientists and developers on one-year contracts to maintain it, and since 2020 LexImpact is a unit of the Assemblée nationale attached to its oversight and evaluation directorate. It lets deputies cost changes to finance bills (a rate-schedule change in about "une minute"), with state-budget (population-level) effects reserved for deputies — [Next INpact](https://next.ink/brief-article/lassemblee-nationale-pousse-leximpact-logiciel-libre-de-simulation-fiscale/); [data.gouv.fr LexImpact](https://www.data.gouv.fr/organizations/leximpact/information); [ModParl profile](https://modparl.substack.com/p/leximpact)
- Mon-entreprise: its engine "Publicodes" began as the engine of an employer-cost simulator that first ran on OpenFisca; the team then won funding and was "internalised" by URSSAF (the social-contribution collector), which led to mon-entreprise.fr; the repository is MIT-licensed and contains rules for social contributions, taxes and social rights — [Genèse de Publicodes](https://hackmd.io/@laem/genese-publicodes); [betagouv/mon-entreprise](https://data.code.gouv.fr/hosts/GitHub/repositories/betagouv%2Fmon-entreprise/dependencies)
- A French public-investment bank article on "start-ups d'État" frames scaling beyond the incubator as the central unresolved challenge — [Banque des Territoires](https://www.banquedesterritoires.fr/start-detat-le-defi-du-passage-lechelle)

**New Zealand**
- Better Rules began in early 2018 with Inland Revenue, MBIE, the Parliamentary Counsel Office and a private software firm; the Service Innovation Lab encoded entitlements for SmartStart (a reusable "legislation as code" component used by three life-event services), ran a Rates Rebate implementation in parallel, and used OpenFisca for citizenship and ACC provisions; by 2019 Better Rules had become an MBIE-led programme "supported by" the Lab — [Service Innovation Lab project page](https://serviceinnovationlab.github.io/projects/legislation-as-code); [Better Rules discovery report, digital.govt.nz](https://www.digital.govt.nz/showcase/better-rules-for-government-discovery-report/)
- The Lab's own stated lesson: coding rules after drafting is risky because implementation drifts from intent, and the SmartStart coded legislation itself became "a siloed version of the rules" — the problem Better Rules was meant to solve — [Service Innovation Lab project page](https://serviceinnovationlab.github.io/projects/legislation-as-code)
- The NZ Law Foundation funded (NZ$101,521) an independent study, "Legislation as Code for New Zealand" (Barraclough, Fraser, Barnes, March 2021). Its headline: "legislation should not be code, but law as code is the future"; enacting code as legislation would risk constitutional arrangements and confer too much power on the Executive; coded versions must stay subordinate to written law; the "better rules" drafting approach is the promising part — [NZ Law Foundation](https://lawfoundation.org.nz/?p=11610); [Scoop, March 2021](https://www.scoop.co.nz/stories/AK2103/S00275/legislation-should-not-be-code-but-law-as-code-is-the-future-legal-researchers.htm)
- The Rates Rebate scheme itself continues to be amended through ordinary text instruments (annual Specified Amounts Orders, e.g. 2026 order lifting the maximum rebate from $805 to $830; Rates Rebate Amendment Act 2025, assented 27 June 2025), with no sign in those instruments of an official coded version — [Rates Rebate (Specified Amounts) Order 2026](https://www.legislation.govt.nz/secondary-legislation/pco-drafted/2026/84/en/latest/); [Rates Rebate Amendment Act 2025](https://www.legislation.govt.nz/act/public/2025/31/en/latest/)

**Canada**
- The Canada School of Public Service ran a rules-as-code discovery project on vacation-pay entitlements (ss. 12-13 Canada Labour Standards Regulations) with ESDC's labour programme and a private-sector partner who programmed the decision tree into a proof-of-concept calculator. Stated lessons: converting rules to code "is not as simple as handing a ruleset to a developer"; even prescriptive rules were hard (experts had to be consulted on how medical leave affected the calculation); the most valuable part was the learning; the process can produce better-drafted rules — [CSPS video](https://www.csps-efpc.gc.ca/video/rules-as-code-2-eng.aspx); [McNaughton slides, CIAJ 2020](https://ciaj-icaj.ca/wp-content/uploads/documents/2020/11/ld259_rules-as-code_scott-mcnaughton.pdf?id=12754)
- Related: a Code for Canada fellowship with ESDC built a "Policy Difference Engine" to measure the impact of rule changes; a former ESDC rules-as-code director published lessons on using OpenFisca for Service Canada expert systems — [Code for Canada](https://codefor.ca/blog/introducing-the-policy-difference-engine); [Jason Morris, 2 Mar 2022](https://gauntlet173.github.io/post/2022-03-02_openfisca_expert_systems)

**Australia**
- New South Wales (Department of Customer Service) published a machine-readable version of the Community Gaming Regulation 2020 on an OpenFisca-based platform "in tandem with" the human-readable regulation, exposed by API, reported in October 2020 as an Australian first; method was manual translation of prescriptive rules, publication, and testing the interpretation "with clear, reproducible test data" — [iTnews](https://www.itnews.com.au/news/nsw-govt-makes-its-first-regulation-machine-readable-554167); [digital.nsw.gov.au](https://www.digital.nsw.gov.au/article/an-australian-first-nsw-translating-rules-as-code-to-make-compliance-easy)
- Robodebt: the Royal Commission (report 7 July 2023) found the scheme unlawful, "neither fair nor legal"; income averaging of annual tax-office data across fortnights was the core defect, and the department did not even track which debts came from averaging. The scheme was meant to save A$4.7bn but delivered A$406m and cost A$971m (figures as reported by the Canberra Times); a A$1.8bn class-action settlement was approved by the Federal Court in June 2021; over A$750m was wrongfully recovered from about 381,000 people — [Canberra Times](https://www.canberratimes.com.au/story/8261218/robodebt-figures-to-face-civil-and-criminal-prosecution/); [Community Legal Centres Australia](https://clcs.org.au/robodebt-royal-commission-report-unravels-systemic-injustice-and-recommends-urgent-reform/); [The Mandarin](https://stagecdn.themandarin.com.au/160061-robodebt-failures-should-have-been-obvious-to-senior-public-servants-judge-finds)
- A further Robodebt settlement of A$548.5m was reported in 2025 (secondary, not verified against a primary source) — [The Register AU](https://theregisterau.substack.com/p/government-to-pay-5485-million-in)

**OECD "Cracking the Code" (OPSI, 12 Oct 2020, Mohun and Roberts; OECD Working Papers on Public Governance No. 42)**
- Defines RaC as "a machine-consumable version of some types of government rules" existing alongside the natural-language version; calls it "an early stage concept with much to test"; no known large-scale approach embedded across a government — [OECD OPSI](https://oecd-opsi.org/publications/cracking-the-code/); [OECD](https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/cracking-the-code_3afe6ba5-en)
- Claimed benefits: quicker service delivery, more consistent application, lower compliance burden, better ex ante modelling, and economic benefit from publishing rules "as a public good" for third parties to consume — [OECD OPSI](https://oecd-opsi.org/publications/cracking-the-code/)
- Flagged open problems: "unresolved questions about the legal status of machine-consumable laws", how versions should be governed, and how errors in coded rules are identified and corrected. Proposed principles: transparency, accountability, traceability, appropriateness, appealability, availability, interoperability, security — [OECD OPSI](https://oecd-opsi.org/publications/cracking-the-code/)
- Jersey (not in the brief but the most active drafting-office project found): its Legislative Drafting Office runs a Computer-Readable Legislation Project to have drafters produce computer-readable versions of the logical structure of draft legislation, with a 2026 States Assembly report — [States Assembly R.24/2026](https://www.statesassembly.je/publications/assembly-reports/2026/r-24-2026); [project launch note](https://digitallegislation.substack.com/p/launching-the-computer-readable-legislation)

### Inferences
- The French sequence is the best template for Colorado's question: (1) compelled transparency of the agency's own calculator, (2) an independent open model (OpenFisca) maintained outside the tax authority, (3) institutional adoption by the legislature (LexImpact) and by an agency for guidance (Mon-entreprise). None of the three made code legally authoritative.
- The DGFiP's objection that code reveals its readings of doctrine is an argument for publication (hidden interpretation) as much as against it; the same objection would likely be raised by the Colorado Department of Revenue.
- Publishing agency code is not sufficient: the French M code was unusable without academic tooling, so "open" has to include runnability, tests and documentation.
- Pilots housed in innovation labs without a statutory or line-agency owner (NZ Service Innovation Lab, CSPS, NSW) tend to end as demonstrations. Institutional absorption, not technical success, predicts survival.
- Robodebt is not an argument against publishing rules as code; it is an argument against automating adverse decisions on an untested legal interpretation with no human review and no error tracking. Open, testable code would have exposed the averaging rule earlier.

### Gaps
- No independent outcome evaluation, cost figure or usage data was found for the NZ Rates Rebate pilot, the NSW Community Gaming rules, or the CSPS pilot; I could not confirm whether the NSW OpenFisca API is still maintained in 2026 or what became of the NZ Service Innovation Lab after 2019.
- No budget figures found for OpenFisca, LexImpact or Mon-entreprise.
- OECD primer was read via its landing page only; chapter-level recommendations (multidisciplinary teams, which rules are suitable) were not verified from the PDF.
- Robodebt figures come from press coverage of the Royal Commission report, not the report itself.

## 2. What have US states and the federal government done?

### Takeaway
The US federal experiment (IRS Direct File) was technically successful, published its rules engine ("Fact Graph") as public-domain code, and was then shut down politically in November 2025, stranding states that had built integrations; Colorado, which had been due to join for the 2026 season, fell back to a narrow Code for America tool (SimpleFile, July 2026). US rules-as-code work is otherwise concentrated in benefits (Beeck Center, Nava, Code for America, PolicyEngine/MyFriendBen); I found no US state revenue department publishing its income-tax calculation logic and no US state legislature piloting rules as code alongside bills.

### Cited Findings

**IRS Direct File**
- Piloted in 12 states in 2024, 25 states in 2025; the IRS told states by email on 3 November 2025 that Direct File was closed with no future launch date; federal officials cited low uptake and private alternatives — [Route Fifty, Sept 2026](https://www.route-fifty.com/digital-government/2026/09/state-efforts-fill-void-left-federal-direct-file-are-underway/416234/); [Texas Society of CPAs, 14 Nov 2025](https://www.tx.cpa/news-publications/news-announcements/article/2025/11/14/irs-suspends-direct-file-program-texas-participated-in-multi-state-pilot)
- 2025 usage: about 296,500 federal returns (about 0.2 percent of returns); an internal IRS report obtained by NextgovFCW had 94 percent of surveyed users rating it "excellent" or "above average"; Treasury put 2025 cost at $41 million — [Route Fifty, Sept 2026](https://www.route-fifty.com/digital-government/2026/09/state-efforts-fill-void-left-federal-direct-file-are-underway/416234/); [Craig Daily Press](https://www.craigdailypress.com/news/gov-polis-trump-administration-reinstate-tax-filing-2026/)
- Conflicting figure: Governor Polis's 3 December 2025 letter to Treasury said "more than 750,000 taxpayers in 25 states" used Direct File, $126 million in refunds, $7.8 million in preparation fees saved — [Craig Daily Press](https://www.craigdailypress.com/news/gov-polis-trump-administration-reinstate-tax-filing-2026/); contradicted on user count by Treasury's "fewer than 300,000 returns" in the same article and [Route Fifty](https://www.route-fifty.com/digital-government/2026/09/state-efforts-fill-void-left-federal-direct-file-are-underway/416234/) (the 750,000 may be cumulative or include other usage; unresolved)
- Colorado was set to begin using Direct File in 2026 and lost it; Polis wrote that "as a federal filing tool, there is no substitute for Direct File"; Colorado then announced a pilot to contact non-filers and offer a pre-filled state return — [Craig Daily Press](https://www.craigdailypress.com/news/gov-polis-trump-administration-reinstate-tax-filing-2026/)
- The IRS published most of the Direct File code on GitHub (reported release 30 May 2025) as a US government work in the public domain; the core is the "Fact Graph", a declarative XML-based knowledge graph in Scala (JVM and Scala.js) designed to reason over incomplete returns; a standalone IRS-Public/fact-graph repository describes it as a production-ready knowledge graph for modelling the Internal Revenue Code. The repositories carry disclaimers that the IRS does not endorse, maintain or guarantee the accuracy of the code — [IRS-Public/direct-file](https://github.com/IRS-Public/direct-file); [FAS, 13 June 2025](https://fas.org/publication/the-direct-file-dream-lives-on/); [GovCIO Media](https://govciomedia.com/?p=31414); [secondary summary of the fact-graph disclaimer](https://feedbagel.com/post/irs-publishes-fact-graph-repository-on-github-with-strong-legal-disclaimer). Note FAS (June 2025) said the IRS "actively maintains" the code, which predates the November 2025 shutdown.
- State responses in 2026: Colorado and New Jersey partnered with Code for America to launch SimpleFile (July 2026); Maryland launched Maryland Tax Connect (September 2026) drawing on Direct File's interview and prepopulation design; Virginia enacted a bill (signed March 2026) requiring a free filing programme by 1 January 2028 and directing the tax department to consider compatibility with "the publicly available source code of the IRS Direct File Program"; Minnesota's May 2026 omnibus tax bill requires a free electronic filing system from 31 December 2026; California's CalFile and Oregon's direct file option continue — [Route Fifty, Sept 2026](https://www.route-fifty.com/digital-government/2026/09/state-efforts-fill-void-left-federal-direct-file-are-underway/416234/); [Philomath News (Oregon)](https://philomathnews.com/trump-administration-axes-federal-free-tax-filing-program-but-oregons-state-option-remains/)
- Stranded-investment criticism: Gabriel Zucker (Economic Security Project) said states "went out on a limb" building connections and "the rug was pulled out from under them"; standalone state tools get low use because most people file state and federal together — [Route Fifty, Sept 2026](https://www.route-fifty.com/digital-government/2026/09/state-efforts-fill-void-left-federal-direct-file-are-underway/416234/); earlier warning in [Route Fifty, April 2025](https://www.route-fifty.com/digital-government/2025/04/ending-direct-file-could-have-downstream-impacts-states-experts-worry/404717)

**Colorado SimpleFile (2026)**
- Free, state-only simplified return built by Code for America with the Colorado Department of Revenue, launched July 2026 on Code for America's GetCTC platform using Direct File design principles; about 15 minutes; aimed at people with no filing obligation so they can claim the Colorado Child Tax Credit and Family Affordability Tax Credit; eligibility: full-year residents with income under $15,000 (under $26,000 married) who had not filed a 2025 return; no income documents required; produces a DR 0104; deadline 16 November 2026 — [Code for America](https://codeforamerica.org/?p=17009); [Colorado Politics, 10 July 2026](https://www.coloradopolitics.com/2026/07/10/colorado-partners-with-tech-organization-to-simplify-tax-filing-process/); [SimpleFile Colorado FAQ](https://www.getyourrefund.org/en/faq/simplefile_colorado); [Boulder County](https://bouldercounty.gov/news/colorado-families-can-claim-up-to-4473-per-child-by-nov-16/)

**Benefits rules as code (US)**
- Georgetown's Beeck Center Digital Benefits Network published "Benefit Eligibility Rules as Code" (Feb 2022), "Exploring Rules Communication: Moving Beyond Static Documents to Standardized Code for U.S. Public Benefits Programs" (5 June 2023), and "AI-Powered Rules as Code: Experiments with Public Benefits Policy" (2025; four experiments on translating SNAP and Medicaid policy to code; Policy2Code challenge with twelve teams). The DBN merged into the Digital Government Network in January 2025 — [Beeck Center 2023 report](https://beeckcenter.georgetown.edu/report/exploring-rules-communication-moving-beyond-static-documents-to-standardized-code-for-u-s-public-benefits-programs/); [Beeck Center AI experiments](https://beeckcenter.georgetown.edu/the-digital-benefits-network-and-massive-data-institute-experiment-with-ai-to-translate-public-benefits-policies-into-software-code/); [Digital Government Hub RaC interest area](https://digitalgovernmenthub.org/get-involved/rules-as-code-interest-area/)
- Colorado-specific: MyFriendBen, a benefits and tax-credit screener incubated at Gary Community Ventures, is open source and relies on PolicyEngine, "a national nonprofit that helps build and maintain open source eligibility rules"; it is used by Adams, Eagle, Jefferson and Denver counties and Colorado 211, received a $2.4M grant and outside philanthropy to expand nationally, and was spun out as a separate entity with its own CEO — [Gary Community Ventures case study](https://garycommunity.org/case-study/myfriendben/); [Gary: $2.4M grant](https://garycommunity.org/?p=6857); [Colorado Sun, 5 Jan 2024](https://coloradosun.com/2024/01/05/my-friend-ben/)

**State revenue departments publishing logic or APIs**
- Colorado already publishes machine-readable sales-tax rules of a kind: a GIS database of rates and jurisdictions inside its Sales and Use Tax System (SUTS), with statutory hold-harmless for vendors who rely on it (see Question 3), and it approves sales-tax software for an XML e-file format — [DOR GIS hold-harmless page](https://tax.colorado.gov/gis-hold-harmless-provision); [DOR Software Developers, Sales Tax XML](https://tax.colorado.gov/software-developers-sales-tax)

**Legislatures**
- I found no US state legislature drafting in executable form or piloting rules as code alongside bills. The closest published US proposal is a 2022 Beeck-affiliated paper envisioning a federal rules-as-code approach — [Envisioning a Rules as Code Approach by the US Federal Government](https://digitalgovernmenthub.org/wp-content/uploads/2022/09/Envisioning-a-Rules-as-Code-Approach-by-the-US-Federal-Government.pdf)

### Inferences
- Direct File shows that the weak point of an agency-owned model is political, not technical: one administration can end it. It also shows the value of open publication as an insurance policy: the Fact Graph outlived the programme and is now referenced in Virginia statute.
- Colorado has a live relationship (DOR plus Code for America) and a live open-source Colorado rules base (PolicyEngine via MyFriendBen); a stewardship design can build on those rather than start from nothing.
- The SUTS/GIS precedent means Colorado has already legislated the hard question (what happens when a taxpayer relies on wrong state-published machine data) for sales tax.

### Gaps
- No evidence found of any US state revenue department publishing income-tax calculation logic as code or a public calculation API. Absence of evidence after limited searching; not a confirmed negative.
- Nava's rules-as-code work was not separately researched; no source collected.
- SimpleFile usage numbers, cost and funding source for Colorado were not found; whether its Colorado credit logic is published open source was not confirmed.
- Whether Direct File's repositories are still maintained after November 2025 was not confirmed.

## 3. What is the legal status of code as an interpretation of statute?

### Takeaway
Nowhere surveyed does code have force of law; it is at most informal guidance. In Colorado the only guidance that binds the Department of Revenue is a private letter ruling, and only for its recipient, so a published code base would by default sit at or below the level of a non-binding general information letter unless the legislature creates a statutory safe harbour, as it did for the sales-tax GIS database. Scholarship (Blank and Osofsky; Lawsky; the NZ Law Foundation study) converges on: publish the code, keep text supreme, protect good-faith reliers from penalties, and expose the interpretive choices.

### Cited Findings

**Colorado guidance hierarchy**
- Private letter rulings are "generally binding upon the Department, but may only be relied upon by the taxpayer to whom they are issued"; they require a fee (the department's request materials cite $500). General information letters are non-binding statements of established interpretations not applied to specific facts, free, normally answered within 120 days. Governing rule: 1 CCR 201-1, Rule 24-35-103.5 — [DOR: Requesting a GIL or PLR](https://tax.colorado.gov/requesting-a-general-information-letter-or-private-letter-ruling); [DOR letter rulings](https://tax.colorado.gov/letter-rulings); [DOR request guide, June 2025](https://tax.colorado.gov/sites/tax/files/documents/Requesting_a_GIL_or_PLR_June_2025.pdf)
- Published PLRs state they bind the Department only as to the specific taxpayer and facts and "CANNOT be relied upon by any other taxpayer" — example text at [CO PLR 16-015 (third-party copy)](https://ezel.ai/tax-rulings/co/plr-16-015-acquisition-and-restructuring-transaction)
- Informal guidance can be withdrawn wholesale: the Department rescinded all prior revenue bulletins and policy positions in 2018 (secondary) — [JD Supra](https://jdsupra.com/legalnews/colorado-department-of-revenue-shocks-32907)

**Colorado's existing statutory reliance safe harbour for machine-readable tax data**
- A vendor that uses the DOR GIS database to determine rate and jurisdiction is held harmless, in an audit by the Department or any local taxing jurisdiction, for tax "that otherwise would be due solely as a result of an error or omission in the GIS database data", provided it can "collect, retain, and produce upon request documentation reasonably sufficient to demonstrate" reliance; a vendor that queries with an incomplete or erroneous address "shall not be held harmless" — [DOR GIS hold-harmless page](https://tax.colorado.gov/gis-hold-harmless-provision)
- Statutory basis sits in C.R.S. 39-26-105.2 and 39-26-105.3 ("electronic database - vendor held harmless"); SB24-023 (2024) extended the protection so local taxing authorities must hold taxpayers harmless for underpayments resulting solely from GIS errors, effective 1 July 2025 (effective date per secondary summary) — [C.R.S. 39-26-105.3](https://colorado.public.law/statutes/crs_39-26-105.3); [SB24-023 text](https://leg.colorado.gov/sites/default/files/documents/2024A/bills/2024a_023_01.pdf); [Legislative history of SUTS](https://leg.colorado.gov/sites/default/files/images/suts_system_history.pdf)

**Estoppel**
- Colorado equitable estoppel generally requires four elements including detrimental, reasonable reliance on the other party's representation (general Colorado law; I found no Colorado appellate decision applying it against the Department of Revenue) — [Colorado Supreme Court opinion via Leagle](https://www.leagle.com/decision/incoco20190624051)
- Comparators show estoppel against tax agencies is narrow and often statutory: New Mexico limits it to reliance on a regulation or a written ruling addressed to the taxpayer (NMSA 7-1-60), with oral employee advice insufficient; Arizona's Supreme Court treated the question in Valencia Energy v. Arizona Department of Revenue — [NM decision 10-12](https://www.tax.newmexico.gov/all-nm-taxes/wp-content/uploads/sites/9/2021/03/10-12__adobe_rose_b_and_b.pdf); [Valencia Energy (Ariz. 1998)](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/court/az-supreme-court/1086996.html)

**Disclaimers in practice**
- The IRS's own open-source release disclaims endorsement, maintenance and accuracy of the Direct File/Fact Graph code — [IRS-Public/direct-file](https://github.com/IRS-Public/direct-file); [secondary summary](https://feedbagel.com/post/irs-publishes-fact-graph-repository-on-github-with-strong-legal-disclaimer)

**Scholarship**
- Blank and Osofsky, "Automated Legal Guidance", 106 Cornell L. Rev. 179 (2020): agency tools such as the IRS Interactive Tax Assistant are cheap and easy but give less precise and sometimes inaccurate legal positions; they rely on "simplexity" (complex law presented as simple without simplifying the law). Recommendations: stronger oversight and review of automated guidance; let people avoid certain penalties when they relied on it; guard against widening the advice gap between high- and low-income people — [Cornell Law Review](https://publications.lawschool.cornell.edu/lawreview/?p=2653); [JOTWELL review](https://adlaw.jotwell.com/?p=2630); follow-up chapter "Simplexity in Automated Legal Guidance" in Automated Agencies (Cambridge, 2025), based on an ACUS-supported survey of federal agencies 2019-2023 — [Cambridge](https://resolve.cambridge.org/core/books/automated-agencies/simplexity-in-automated-legal-guidance/61D6136C358B1DE26883B206BA185F8B)
- Lawsky, "Coding the Code: Catala and Computationally Accessible Tax Law", 75 SMU L. Rev. 535 (2022): government already turns law into algorithms through forms and worksheets, so "the difficult part of the coding, and the judgment calls, are almost entirely performed by the government"; forms themselves embed judgments about unclear law; an open formalisation is more transparent than the current "patchwork of forms, worksheets, and secret programs". Formalising IRC s.121 with Protzenko surfaced fine interpretive disputes even for an expert — [TaxProf Blog / Grimmelmann review](https://taxprofblog.aals.org/2024/08/14/grimmelmann-reviews-lawskys-coding-the-code-catala-and-computationally-accessible-tax-law/); [CACM, Converting Laws to Programs](https://cacm.acm.org/news/converting-laws-to-programs)
- NZ Law Foundation study (2021): code must remain subordinate to enacted text; enacting code shifts power to the executive — [Scoop](https://www.scoop.co.nz/stories/AK2103/S00275/legislation-should-not-be-code-but-law-as-code-is-the-future-legal-researchers.htm)
- French tax authority's own stated concern (2015): source code can expose the administration's particular readings of doctrine — [Next INpact](https://next.ink/news/95159-le-fisc-rechigne-a-)
- OECD: legal status, version governance and error correction of coded rules are unresolved — [OECD OPSI](https://oecd-opsi.org/publications/cracking-the-code/)

### Inferences
- Default Colorado status for any published tax code base: informational, non-binding, no reliance protection (weaker than a PLR, comparable to a GIL or a form instruction). A DOR-owned code base would raise the reliance question more sharply than a nonprofit or university one, because it is the agency's own representation.
- The GIS hold-harmless statute gives a ready drafting model for an income-tax analogue: protection limited to errors "solely" in the state artefact, conditioned on documented, correct-input use, covering tax/penalty/interest as the legislature chooses. Blank and Osofsky's narrower proposal (penalty relief only) is the lower-cost variant.
- Lawsky's point undercuts the "code would bind the agency" objection: form DR 0104 and its worksheets are already an algorithm embodying DOR interpretations; code makes them inspectable rather than creating new commitments.
- Risk on the other side: a single official encoding can freeze one reading of ambiguous provisions and remove discretion; mitigations in the literature are text supremacy, documented interpretive choices, appeal routes and human review for adverse actions.

### Gaps
- No Colorado case law located on estoppel or reliance specifically against the Department of Revenue, and no Colorado statute found giving a general penalty defence for reliance on written DOR advice; needs a legal database search.
- The exact current subsection of C.R.S. 39-26-105.2/105.3 governing the hold-harmless, and whether it covers tax, penalty and interest, was not read in the official text.
- I did not find Colorado-specific disclaimers or liability rules for state-published calculators, or governmental-immunity analysis (Colorado Governmental Immunity Act) for erroneous software.
- Lawsky's "Formalizing the Code" and Blank and Osofsky's full texts were not read; summaries come from reviews and abstracts.

## 4. Stewardship models and funding: pros, cons, what has lasted and what collapsed

### Takeaway
Evidence favours splitting roles rather than picking one owner: an independent open code base (nonprofit/university-maintained) that an official body validates or adopts, because agency-only ownership is exposed to political reversal and reorganisation (Direct File 2025; Colorado OIT's 2026 layoffs) and nonprofit-only ownership is exposed to funding collapse (Benefits Data Trust 2024). The projects that lasted were absorbed onto a permanent institutional budget (Assemblée nationale for LexImpact, URSSAF for Mon-entreprise).

### Cited Findings

**State agency (DOR / OIT / Colorado Digital Service)**
- Pro: the agency already makes the interpretive judgment calls through forms (Lawsky) and holds the taxpayer data needed for validation — [TaxProf Blog](https://taxprofblog.aals.org/2024/08/14/grimmelmann-reviews-lawskys-coding-the-code-catala-and-computationally-accessible-tax-law/)
- Con (political fragility): Direct File was closed by a change of administration after two seasons despite 94 percent high satisfaction — [Route Fifty](https://www.route-fifty.com/digital-government/2026/09/state-efforts-fill-void-left-federal-direct-file-are-underway/416234/)
- Con (reluctance and usability): the DGFiP resisted disclosure, and its published M code needed outside academic tooling to be usable — [Next INpact](https://next.ink/news/95159-le-fisc-rechigne-a-); [arXiv 2011.07966](https://arxiv.org/abs/2011.07966v3)
- Colorado Digital Service: formed October 2019 inside the Governor's Office of Information Technology, modelled on the US Digital Service; a cross-functional team of senior engineers, designers, product managers and procurement specialists that partners with agencies to "design, build, and buy" technology — [OIT: Colorado Digital Service](https://oit.colorado.gov/colorado-digital-service)
- Instability in 2026: in March 2026 OIT proposed a reorganisation into "Digital and Delivery" and "Security and Infrastructure" teams (framed as scaling CDS); in late May 2026 the state overhauled OIT and laid off 173 employees after negative agency feedback; Sarah Tuneberg, who had led CDS, took over OIT leadership in June 2026 — [KUNC, 29 May 2026](https://www.kunc.org/politics/2026-05-29/colorado-overhauls-state-it-office-lays-off-173-employees-after-negative-feedback); [Colorado Sun, 27 May 2026](https://coloradosun.com/2026/05/27/colorado-overhauls-state-it-office-lays-off-employees/); [OIT operating model, JTC June 2026](https://content.leg.colorado.gov/sites/default/files/JTC%20June%202026_%20OIT%27s%20Operating%20Model.pdf)

**Legislature (Legislative Council Staff / OLLS)**
- Precedent: LexImpact has been a unit of the French National Assembly since 2020, attached to its oversight and evaluation directorate, after starting as a DINUM incubator project; hiring was authorised by the chamber's own administration — [Next INpact](https://next.ink/brief-article/lassemblee-nationale-pousse-leximpact-logiciel-libre-de-simulation-fiscale/); [data.gouv.fr](https://www.data.gouv.fr/organizations/leximpact/information)
- Colorado fit: Legislative Council Staff fiscal notes already estimate tax-credit costs bill by bill from Department of Revenue data and income distributions (e.g. assuming 130,000 claimants for a 2024 senior housing credit; share of filers under income thresholds for a childcare-worker credit) — [Fiscal note, senior housing credit](https://leg.colorado.gov/sites/default/files/images/committees/fiscal_note_bill_10_-_senior_housing.pdf); [HB24-1312 fiscal note](https://leg.colorado.gov/sites/default/files/documents/2024A/bills/fn/2024a_hb1312_r1.pdf)
- Con: constitutional caution from NZ — who writes the code gains interpretive power; the NZ study worried about the executive, and the mirror-image concern (legislative staff code read as statements of intent) follows by analogy — [Scoop](https://www.scoop.co.nz/stories/AK2103/S00275/legislation-should-not-be-code-but-law-as-code-is-the-future-legal-researchers.htm)

**University**
- Precedents: IPP (Paris School of Economics-affiliated) co-develops OpenFisca and builds TAXIPP on it; Catala came from Inria/academic collaborators with Lawsky; the Beeck Center (Georgetown) convenes US rules-as-code work — [IPP TAXIPP guide](https://halshs.archives-ouvertes.fr/halshs-02514276v1/file/guide-method-ipp-nov2019-TAXIPP-1-1.pdf); [Northwestern Law on Lawsky and Catala](https://news.law.northwestern.edu/news/sarah-lawsky-worked-on-a-tax-law-code-that-the-french-government-deemed-officially-awesome/); [Beeck Center](https://beeckcenter.georgetown.edu/report/exploring-rules-communication-moving-beyond-static-documents-to-standardized-code-for-u-s-public-benefits-programs/)
- Value shown: independent academic re-encoding caught a bug in the official French family-benefits implementation — [arXiv 2103.03198](https://arxiv.org/abs/2103.03198v2)
- Con: university centres themselves reorganise (Digital Benefits Network merged into the Digital Government Network in January 2025) — [Digital Government Hub](https://digitalgovernmenthub.org/get-involved/rules-as-code-interest-area/)

**Nonprofit / civic tech**
- Working Colorado example: PolicyEngine maintains open-source eligibility rules consumed by MyFriendBen, funded by Gary Community Ventures and later outside philanthropy; MyFriendBen was spun out as its own entity — [Gary case study](https://garycommunity.org/case-study/myfriendben/); [Gary $2.4M grant](https://garycommunity.org/?p=6857)
- Working Colorado example: Code for America built SimpleFile with DOR in 2026 — [Code for America](https://codeforamerica.org/?p=17009)
- Collapse example: Benefits Data Trust (Philadelphia benefits-access nonprofit, about 20 years old) announced in late June 2024 it would wind down in 60 days and closed 24 August 2024, laying off all 273 employees, after revenue grew from about $12M (2017) to nearly $46M (2022), a $20M unrestricted MacKenzie Scott gift (2022) and a projected $9.8M loss for 2023; no acquirer was found; states that depended on it were left exposed — [Philadelphia Inquirer](https://www.inquirer.com/health/benefits-data-trust-bdt-failed-acquirer-closing-august-24-20240730.html); [Chronicle of Philanthropy](https://www.philanthropy.com/news/why-20-million-from-mackenzie-scott-couldnt-save-this-a-i-nonprofit/); [Route Fifty, July 2024](https://route-fifty.com/management/2024/07/nonprofits-abrupt-closure-puts-access-public-benefits-risk/397968); [WHYY](https://whyy.org/articles/philadelphia-benefits-data-trust-closure-employees-laid-off)

**Funding patterns that sustained comparable projects**
- Incubator money then absorption onto a permanent institutional budget: LexImpact (DINUM start-up d'État to National Assembly staff), Mon-entreprise (beta.gouv to URSSAF funding and internalisation) — [Next INpact](https://next.ink/brief-article/lassemblee-nationale-pousse-leximpact-logiciel-libre-de-simulation-fiscale/); [Genèse de Publicodes](https://hackmd.io/@laem/genese-publicodes)
- Research-foundation grants for one-off studies: NZ Law Foundation NZ$101,521 — [NZ Law Foundation](https://lawfoundation.org.nz/?p=11610)
- Philanthropy for US civic tech: Gary Community Ventures and others for MyFriendBen/PolicyEngine — [Gary](https://garycommunity.org/?p=6857)
- Federal appropriations/agency budget: Direct File at $41M in 2025, ended by executive decision — [Craig Daily Press](https://www.craigdailypress.com/news/gov-polis-trump-administration-reinstate-tax-filing-2026/)
- Scaling out of the incubator is the recognised failure point for French state start-ups — [Banque des Territoires](https://www.banquedesterritoires.fr/start-detat-le-defi-du-passage-lechelle)

### Inferences
- Summary of trade-offs by model (my synthesis from the findings above):
  - DOR: highest authority and data access; lowest independence; incentive to avoid being pinned to interpretations; dependent on its core tax-system vendor; exposed to administration change.
  - OIT/CDS: delivery skill and procurement expertise but no tax-law authority, and 2026 turmoil makes it a risky sole home.
  - Legislative Council Staff/OLLS: natural consumer (fiscal notes, drafting) and nonpartisan; the LexImpact path shows it can work; but capacity is seasonal and staff code could be misread as legislative intent.
  - University (e.g. CU / Silicon Flatirons): independence, formal-methods and legal scholarship, cheap labour; weak on annual operational deadlines and continuity.
  - Nonprofit (PolicyEngine, Code for America, Colorado Fiscal Institute, Bell Policy Center): existing code and speed; perceived policy alignment for advocacy groups, and grant dependence (BDT).
  - Multi-stakeholder foundation: spreads political and funding risk and matches how OpenFisca is used (state, parliament, researchers on one code base); costs are governance overhead and diffuse accountability for errors.
- A defensible Colorado design is layered: open code base maintained by a nonprofit/university consortium; DOR supplies test cases and reviews (or certifies) releases; Legislative Council Staff consumes it for fiscal notes; a line-item appropriation or statutory duty provides the permanent floor that philanthropy cannot.
- Open licensing is the hedge against every failure mode: Direct File's code survived its programme; BDT's services did not survive its organisation.

### Gaps
- PolicyEngine's funder list and budget were not found (only that it is a nonprofit with philanthropic support and a GitHub Sponsors page).
- No sources collected on the Bell Policy Center, Colorado Fiscal Institute, University of Colorado or Silicon Flatirons having done tax-law-as-code work; their inclusion above is analytical, not evidenced.
- No Colorado cost estimate exists in sources found for building or maintaining a state income-tax rules base.
- Whether the December 2025 OIT budget request for a CDS generative-AI team was funded is unconfirmed.

## 5. Relevant Colorado institutions and laws

### Takeaway
Colorado has most of the institutional pieces (a digital service, a statutory data-governance board and open-data portal, a nonpartisan fiscal-note office, an existing e-file developer programme, a statutory reliance safe harbour for machine-readable sales-tax data) but no open-source or rules-as-code mandate that I could find, and two 2026 shocks matter: the OIT overhaul and the repeal-and-replace of the 2024 AI Act by SB26-189 (effective 1 January 2027), which now centres on automated decision-making technology. ### Inferences
- A deterministic rules engine that only computes liability from statute is not obviously "AI", but SB26-189's reported focus on automated decision-making technology and human review means any state use of the code to make or substantially drive adverse determinations (credit denials, assessments) should be designed with explanation, correction and human-review features from the start. Whether the act reaches state agencies at all needs checking in the bill text.
- Amendment 87 is the strongest near-term use case and the hardest test: brackets, any implementing legislation in the 2027 session, withholding tables and form DR 0104 will all change together. An open rules base could let Legislative Council Staff, DOR and vendors work from one tested specification; equally, building against a moving target raises cost.
- The GDAB/open-data framework governs data, not software; publishing rules as code likely needs either an executive direction or a short statute (mandate, licence, disclaimer/safe harbour, owner, appropriation).

### Gaps
- No Colorado open-source software statute, executive order or OIT policy was found (not confirmed absent).
- SB26-189 was not read in primary form: scope (public-sector coverage), definitions of ADMT, exemptions, and reported new litigation are unverified.

## 6. Validation and keeping the code current

### Takeaway
The practical validation route is the one commercial vendors already use: Colorado's MeF-based e-file programme and substitute-form review give an annual, form-anchored acceptance process, and foreign practice adds published reproducible test cases and independent re-implementation, which has repeatedly found errors in official systems. Currency is the expensive part: annual form and parameter changes, plus session law and ballot measures such as the 2026 graduated-tax amendment.

### Cited Findings
- Colorado e-file is an extension of IRS MeF; vendors must meet IRS Publication 1345 plus Colorado-specific handbook rules, updated annually (tax year 2025 handbook revised 13 January 2026) — [DOR Efilers Handbook 2025](https://tax.colorado.gov/sites/tax/files/documents/Efilers_Handbook_2025.docx)
- Substitute-forms review: vendors file an annual Letter of Intent (DR 5029) and submit one PDF per form with samples; DR 0104 is among forms accepted for review; a vendor letter shows Colorado approves e-file support form by form with approval and activation dates — [DOR substitute form developer site](https://sites.google.com/state.co.us/substitute-form-developer/Specifications); [FAQ](https://sites.google.com/state.co.us/substitute-form-developer/FAQ); [Drake Software Colorado letter, TY2023](https://www.drakesoftware.com/sharedassets/letters/2023/state/co.pdf)
- Common pattern in other states: annual Letter of Intent, then access to the Federation of Tax Administrators' State Exchange System for state schemas and test packages, then state Assurance Testing System (ATS) testing (Connecticut, Idaho, Alabama, New York) — [Connecticut DRS](https://portal.ct.gov/DRS/Practitioners/Practitioner-Pages/Software-Developers-Efile); [Idaho Tax Commission](https://tax.idaho.gov/online-services/e-file/e-file-guides/developers/); [New York ATS guidelines](https://www.tax.ny.gov/bus/efile/corp-ats-2025/ats-testing-guidelines.htm); federal ATS scenarios — [IRS Pub 5078](https://www.irs.gov/pub/irs-pdf/p5078.pdf)
- Published reproducible tests as part of the official artefact: NSW tested its interpretation "with clear, reproducible test data" — [digital.nsw.gov.au](https://www.digital.nsw.gov.au/article/an-australian-first-nsw-translating-rules-as-code-to-make-compliance-easy)
- Independent re-implementation and openness find real bugs: DGFiP rounding error found at the 2016 hackathon; Catala found a bug in the official French family-benefits implementation; a later tool (CUTECat) generates test cases for Catala programmes — [Next INpact](https://next.ink/14493/99307-decouverte-dun-bug-dans-code-source-impots/); [arXiv 2103.03198](https://arxiv.org/abs/2103.03198v2); [arXiv 2410.18212](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2410.18212v1)
- Pair programming of lawyer and programmer is Catala's method for controlling interpretive error — [TaxProf Blog](https://taxprofblog.aals.org/2024/08/14/grimmelmann-reviews-lawskys-coding-the-code-catala-and-computationally-accessible-tax-law/)
- Drafting-time approach: Better Rules' lesson is to test implementation alongside policy drafting with multidisciplinary teams; Jersey's drafting office is producing computer-readable logical structure of draft legislation; LexImpact gives deputies amendment costing during finance-bill debate — [Service Innovation Lab](https://serviceinnovationlab.github.io/projects/legislation-as-code); [Jersey R.24/2026](https://www.statesassembly.je/publications/assembly-reports/2026/r-24-2026); [ModParl on LexImpact](https://modparl.substack.com/p/leximpact)
- AI-assisted translation is experimental: the Beeck Center/Massive Data Institute ran four experiments on using AI to translate SNAP and Medicaid policy into code (2025) — [Beeck Center](https://beeckcenter.georgetown.edu/the-digital-benefits-network-and-massive-data-institute-experiment-with-ai-to-translate-public-benefits-policies-into-software-code/)
- Change cadence evidence for Colorado: 2026 session bills creating, repealing, expanding and narrowing many credits at once (per March and May 2026 fiscal notes); 14 statewide ballot measures in November 2026 including a constitutional change to income-tax structure — [Legislative Council fiscal note (2026)](https://leg.colorado.gov/bill_files/115310/download); [Denver7 ballot measure guide](https://www.denver7.com/news/local-news/interactive-breaking-down-colorados-14-ballot-measures)

### Inferences
- A Colorado test suite could be assembled from three layers: (a) DOR's e-file/ATS-style scenarios for DR 0104 and schedules, if DOR will publish them; (b) worked examples in the DR 0104 booklet; (c) differential testing against PolicyEngine's Colorado model and commercial software outputs. Publication of (a) is itself a low-cost transparency step that needs no new code.
- Because Colorado income tax starts from federal taxable income, the Colorado layer is comparatively thin, but it inherits federal change risk and the loss of a maintained federal reference implementation after Direct File.
- "Structured changes alongside bills" has no US state precedent in my findings; the realistic first step is for fiscal-note analysts to run bill parameters through the open model and attach the parameter diff to the fiscal note, as LexImpact does for amendments.

### Gaps
- Colorado's own ATS test scenarios, schemas and whether it distributes them through the FTA State Exchange System were not confirmed; the handbook's testing section was not read.
- No data on the annual person-effort commercial vendors or PolicyEngine spend on Colorado updates.
- No source on TABOR refund mechanisms' annual variability as an encoding burden (known to matter, not sourced here).

## 7. Who opposes or is harmed

### Takeaway
The documented opposition is from commercial tax-preparation firms to government-run filing, not to open rules as such: Intuit and H&R Block lobbied heavily in 2025 while Direct File was being ended, and H&R Block disclosed Direct File as a revenue risk. Tax agencies are the second source of resistance (France's DGFiP on revealing doctrine; the IRS's blanket disclaimers), and legal scholars and the Robodebt record supply the principled objections about hidden interpretation and automation without review.

### Cited Findings
- Lobbying: Intuit spent about $3 million on federal lobbying across all issues in 2025 ($900,000 in Q3); H&R Block $2.4 million in 2025; industry-aligned groups such as the American Coalition for Taxpayer Rights also lobbied on Direct File; filings do not break out Direct File spending; H&R Block's corporate filings warned products like Direct File could adversely affect revenue and profitability — [Bloomberg Government](https://news.bgov.com/daily-tax-report/tax-prep-gop-aligned-groups-lobby-to-end-irs-direct-file); [Bloomberg Tax, lobbying kept up](https://news.bloomberglaw.com/daily-tax-report/direct-file-lobbying-kept-up-despite-looming-end-bgov-tax-1)
- Attribution is contested: ITEP and the American Economic Liberties Project attribute the cancellation to industry lobbying; companies frame their activity as informing policymakers — [ITEP](https://itep.org/trump-administration-officially-ends-popular-irs-direct-file-program/); [American Economic Liberties Project](https://www.economicliberties.us/press-release/trump-administration-hands-tax-filing-back-to-monopolists-by-killing-irs-direct-file/); earlier history of lobbying against free federal filing — [Fortune, April 2023](https://fortune.com/2023/04/17/taxpayer-advocates-irs-free-electronic-tax-filing-system-intuit-hr-block-spent-millions-lobbying-against/amp)

### Inferences
- Commercial interests split by layer. Consumer tax-prep firms are threatened by free filing products that open rules make cheaper to build (Virginia's statute pointing at Direct File code shows the link). Sales-tax engines and smaller software entrants may benefit from an authoritative, free rules feed, as with the GIS database. A Colorado proposal framed as "open specification and test suite" rather than "state filing product" should draw less opposition.
- Tax practitioners' likely concern (being bound by, or exposed for departing from, an official encoding) is inferred from the guidance-hierarchy and reliance findings, not from any practitioner statement I found.
- Publishers of annotated statutes or tax research products were not found to have taken positions.

### Gaps
- No Colorado-specific lobbying record found for or against state free-file, SimpleFile, or open tax rules (e.g. positions of Intuit, H&R Block, the Colorado Society of CPAs, or sales-tax vendors at the General Assembly).
- No statements from the Colorado Department of Revenue on publishing its calculation logic or on being bound by code.
- No evidence gathered on legal publishers' positions.
